# The Swift Heroine and the Melodious One: Two Taras for the Bumtshok

## An introduction for the Dzongsar Drolma Bumtshok

Among the many forms of Tara — the female Buddha of compassion revered across the Tibetan Buddhist world as the swift protector who answers a cry for help before it is even finished — twenty-one are especially beloved. They come to us through the *Homage to the Twenty-One Taras*, a prayer chanted for centuries in monasteries and homes alike, each verse painting one face of her activity: fearless, luminous, fierce, gentle, always immediate. As the Bumtshok gathers voices together to recite her praises a hundred thousand times over, it is fitting to begin, as the prayer itself does, with the first two of these twenty-one — the two who set the tone for everything that follows.

### Tara the Swift, the Heroine

*སྒྲོལ་མ་མྱུར་མ་དཔའ་མོ།*

She is the very first of the twenty-one, and many great teachers have said she is not merely one Tara among many but the single point where the enlightened activity of every Buddha, past and future, converges into one form. Her name says everything at once. "Tara" means the one who ferries beings across the ocean of samsara's suffering to the other shore. "Swift" means that when she acts for someone in need, there is no delay at all — not even the space of an instant. And "Heroine" means she has the unshakeable strength to meet any fear, any obstacle, any force of harm, and overcome it without hesitation.

Her most striking feature is her eyes, described as flashing like lightning — not because they are fierce, but because in a single flicker they take in everything there is to know, so that no being's suffering ever escapes her notice long enough to go unanswered. She is most often shown in red, her expression poised between a smile and a wrathful readiness to act, one hand open in the gesture that grants the highest accomplishment, the other extended in the gesture that grants refuge, holding a blue lotus and a conch shell that spirals to the right.

The story behind her is one of the most touching in the whole tradition. Avalokiteshvara, the Bodhisattva of Compassion, looked out over the endless multitude of beings still trapped in samsara despite lifetimes spent freeing them, and wept. From the lotus that grew out of his tears, Tara arose — and made a vow on the spot: that she would carry out the work of liberating beings swiftly, without pause, and that she would keep doing so in the form of a woman for as long as samsara itself remained unemptied. Her heroism, then, is not aggression — it is a vow kept without wavering, an urgency that never runs out.

### Tara the Melodious, the Treasure of Wisdom

*སྒྲོལ་མ་བློ་གཏེར་དབྱངས་ཅན་མ།*

The second of the twenty-one is described before she is even named. The root verse simply says: homage to her whose face is like a hundred autumn moons gathered into one, blazing with light beyond a thousand clustered stars. Every commentator agrees on this image of luminous, cooling beauty — white as moonlight, brighter than starlight — even though they disagree, delightfully, on what to actually call her. Some know her as the Melodious One, Treasure of Wisdom; others as the Clear and Radiant White One; others simply take the last words of her own root verse, "Blazing with Light," as her name. All of them are pointing at the same figure.

She is shown in white, at complete peace, seated at ease on a lotus and moon seat, one hand granting the highest accomplishment, the other holding a blue lotus marked with a mirror. It is this mirror that carries her activity: light streaming from it dissolves the darkness of ignorance in the mind and opens up wisdom and clarity where confusion had been. In some lineages her practice is also a way of pacifying illness and the harm of unseen obstacles, and of "ransoming" life itself — offering one's own faults and habitual patterns as a substitute, so that love, compassion, and the awakened heart become the truest protection against death.

### Why begin here

Together, these two set the rhythm for the entire cycle of twenty-one: an instant, fearless compassion that never delays, paired with a cool, radiant wisdom that dispels darkness the moment it appears. To recite their praises at the start of a Bumtshok is to invoke both halves of Tara's activity at once — the swiftness to act, and the clarity to see what needs to be done — before the prayer opens outward into the nineteen forms that follow.
