# Mount Meru — རི་རབ།

> Consolidated from the raw claims files listed in `sources:`. Every attestation
> cites a raw claim ID; raw claims cite `1-SOURCES/` segments. This page never
> cites a commentary file directly, and regenerating it never touches `raw/`.
>
> **Gather note:** `meru` is a global cross-cutting topic, not one of the twenty-two
> registered spine slots — there is no `assemble_packet.py` / spine-map packet for
> it. The claims below were located by searching every commentary's
> `2-RAILS/Claims/raw/tree-guided/<id>.md` file for the string རི་རབ, then reading
> each hit's full claim block (and, where a claim's argument continued into an
> adjacent block without repeating རི་རབ itself, that adjacent block as well)
> directly from the raw file. Every quotation on this page is copied
> character-for-character from that read, never retyped from memory.
>
> **Scope note — cross-reference, not re-derivation.** `2-RAILS/Claims/three-worlds.md`
> already consolidates, in full and with its own Consensus/⚑ Divergences, the verse
> in which Tārā's foot-stamping "shakes Meru, Mandara, and Vindhya, and the three
> worlds" (its facet "Shaking the three worlds — Meru, Mandara, Vindhya (homage 17)"),
> including the dispute over whether "the three worlds" there names the mountains
> themselves or a separate netherworld/human/deva triad, and the internal tension
> (in anon-trinle-char) over whether feet-stamping or HŪM's light rays cause the
> shaking. This page does not re-derive that material. It cites the same verse only
> where a claim says something specifically *about Meru* that the three-worlds page
> does not already carry (the mountain's own epithet, the naming of its companion
> peaks, and one further attestation of the causation question); its main weight
> falls on material three-worlds.md does not touch at all: a second, distinct
> struck-mountain verse; Meru's use as a byword for stability; a rejected
> cosmological claim about its summit; a Vedic narrative about it churning the
> ocean; its place among sacred pilgrimage sites; its use as a ritual measure; and
> an iconographic attribute.

## Questions asked

1. What epithet does Meru carry, and which other mountains are named alongside it in the verse describing the shaking of the mountains and the three worlds?
2. Is there a second, distinct root verse in which Tārā strikes Mount Meru and its surrounding continents with her palm and foot, separate from the verse in which her foot-stamping shakes Meru, Mandara, and Vindhya?
3. Do commentaries diverge on what specifically acts upon Meru — feet-stamping, or light rays radiating from the HŪM syllable?
4. Do commentaries diverge on the identity of the second and third mountains named alongside Meru — Mandara and Vindhya, or alternate readings such as the Himalaya or Kailāśa?
5. Does any commentary explain why the text names Mount Meru specifically, rather than leaving its shaking implicit within "the three worlds"?
6. Does any commentary reject a cosmological application involving Mount Meru, such as measuring the underworld's seven levels from its summit?
7. Does any commentary give Mount Meru a hidden, tantric (completion-stage) meaning distinct from the general "three worlds as channels" reading?
8. Does any commentary supply narrative material about Mount Meru outside the shaking-verse context, such as a cosmogonic myth?
9. Does any commentary place Mount Meru among the world's sacred or pilgrimage sites?
10. Is Mount Meru used as a unit of comparison or measure in ritual/offering contexts elsewhere in the corpus?
11. Does any commentary give Mount Meru an iconographic role, such as being held as a hand-implement by a specific emanation of Tārā?

## Identity — "king of mountains," named among the peaks Tārā's power moves

### Consensus
Twelve commentaries, glossing the verse in which Tārā's foot-stamping shakes Meru, Mandara, and Vindhya (fully treated on `2-RAILS/Claims/three-worlds.md`), independently name Meru specifically — most giving it the standing epithet "king of mountains" (རིའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རི་རབ) — as the first of the mountains her power moves:

— attested: `anon-trinle-char:c-3-17-8`, `anon-utpala:c-1-2-3-2-2`, `anon-utpala:c-1-2-3-2-5`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-2-3`, `gendun-gyatso:c-1-17-1`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-17-2`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-3-2-4`, `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-47`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-18-0-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-18-1-3`, `sungrab-tulku:c-20-4`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-2-4`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-17-4`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-59` (12 commentaries)

`anon-utpala:c-1-2-3-2-5` gives the epithet in its clearest form: "རིའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རི་རབ་དང་། རི་མནྟཱ་ར་དང་། རི་ནག་པོ་འབིགས་བྱེད་ལ་སོགས་པ...གཡོ་བར་མཛད་པ" ("she causes the king of mountains Meru, Mount Mandara, the black mountain Vindhya, and so forth...to shake"). `sungrab-tulku:c-20-4` and `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-17-4` restate the same epithet nearly word for word: "རིའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རི་རབ་དང་། རི་མནྡར་དང་། རི་བོ་ནག་པོ་འབིགས་བྱེད་ལ་སོགས་པ." `sangye-nyentrul:c-18-0-2` and `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-47` likewise open with "རིའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རི་རབ." A closely related epithet — "Mount Meru, king of mountains, abode of the world's great gods" — recurs in `palden-sherab`'s own gloss of the same verse, cited below under ⚑ Divergences for the further naming question it raises about the second and third mountains. `gendun-gyatso:c-1-17-1` uses a distinctive verb for what happens to Meru, "འཕགས་པར་བྱེད" ("causes [it] to be uplifted/exalted") rather than the more common "shakes," while still treating it as the same act. `anon-trinle-char:c-3-17-8` corroborates the naming from the tantra's own wording: "རྒྱུད་རང་ལས། རི་རབ་མནྡར་དང་འབིགས་བྱེད། ། འཇིག་རྟེན་གསུམ་རྣམས" ("the tantra itself: 'Meru, Mandara, and Vindhya — the three worlds'"), which this page cites only for its confirmation that "Meru" is one of the three named mountains — the surrounding dispute over what "the three worlds" itself denotes at this verse is `three-worlds.md`'s territory, not re-argued here.

### ⚑ Divergences
Two distinct disagreements surface once the verse's naming is examined closely — neither is discussed on `three-worlds.md`:

**(a) What acts upon Meru.** The consensus above treats Tārā's feet-stamping (born from the HŪM seed-syllable) as the cause of Meru's shaking. `drakpa-gyaltsen`, however, glosses the same phrase as Meru being *pierced by light*, not struck by feet: `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-86` — "རི་རབ་མན་དྷཱ་ར་དང་འབིགས་བྱེད...སྟོང་གསུམ་གྱི་ཕྱི་རོལ་ན་རི་རབ་མན་དཱ་ར་བ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ཡང་ཡི་གེ་ཧཱུཾ་གི་འོད་ཟེར་གྱིས་འབིགས་པར་བྱེད་པའོ" ("'Meru Mandara and Vibhedaka'...outside the trichiliocosm there is [a mountain] called Meru Mandara, which too is pierced by the light rays of the letter HŪM"). A second claim, this one internal to `anon-trinle-char`'s own commentary (its wider internal tension over the shaking's cause is recorded on `three-worlds.md` at `anon-trinle-char:c-3-17-6`), gives a Meru-specific variant of the same dispute: Nyima Sbaspa's verse, quoted at `anon-trinle-char:c-3-17-10`, reads the HŪM-seed itself as "the cause by which she moves Mount Meru" — "ཧཱུཾ་ཡིག་ས་བོན་ལས་སྐྱེས་མགོན། ། རི་རབ་ཀུན་བསྐྱོད་དབང་སྡུད་བསྒོམ" ("Protector born from the HŪM-seed — meditate on her moving Meru entirely and drawing it under power") — against a rival, unattributed reading (quoted in the same claim) that takes "seed of HŪM's form" merely as a description of Tārā's own wrathful nature, with no reference to Meru at all. Both readings agree Meru moves; they disagree on what makes it move.

**(b) The identity of the second and third mountains.** The majority above names Mandara and Vindhya (འབིགས་བྱེད) as Meru's companions. Three commentaries complicate this:
- `karma-maitri` reads the third name not as an independent peak called Vindhya, but as a *further form of Meru itself*: its own Places register lists "རི་རབ་མནྡ་ར" (Meru Mandara) and "རི་རབ་འབིག་བྱེད" (Meru Vibhedaka) as two "Meru"-prefixed mountain names side by side, and `karma-maitri:c-1-1-17-5` states the pair together: "དེས་སྟོང་གསུམ་གྱི་ཕྱི་རོལ་ན་གནས་རི་རབ་མནྡ་ར་ཞེས་པ་དང་རི་རབ་འབིག་བྱེད་ལ་སོགས་པ་སྟེ" ("by that, she shakes [the mountains] called Meru Mandara and Meru Vibhedaka, and so forth, situated outside the trichiliocosm").
- `taranatha` names the third mountain not as Vindhya but as the Himalaya, home of Maheśvara: `taranatha:c-17-5` — "རིའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རི་རབ་དང་། མནྡ་ར་ཞེས་བྱ་བའི་ར་དང་། རི་བོ་གངས་ཅན་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་དང་དབང་ཕྱུག་ཆེན་པོ་གནས་པའི་རི" ("the king of mountains, Mount Meru, and the mountain called Mandara, and the mountain called the Himalaya, where Maheśvara dwells"). `taranatha:c-17-7` then addresses the variance as a manuscript question in its own right: "བོད་དཔེ་ཕལ་ཆེར་ལས། འབིགས་བྱེད་ཅེས་འབྱུང་བ་ལྟར་ན...གཞུང་འདིའི་རྒྱ་སྐད་མཐོང་པ་རྣམས་ན། ཀཻ་ལ་ཤ་ཅེས་པར་སྣང་སྟེ...དཔེ་སྣ་ཚོགས་པ་སྣང་བའི་ཕྱིར...གཉིས་ཀ་ལྟར་མི་འགལ་བར་བྱའོ" ("according to most Tibetan copies, which read 'Vibhedaka'...those who have seen the Sanskrit of this text find it reads 'Kailāśa'...since various readings appear...both should be taken as not contradicting one another") — treating "Vindhya/Vibhedaka" and "Kailāśa/Himalaya" as two textually attested names for the same third mountain, rather than adjudicating between them.
- `palden-sherab:c-3-1-17-1-2` offers the same two options side by side without choosing: "རིའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རི་རབ་དང་། རི་མནྡཱ་ར་དང་། རི་འབིགས་བྱེད་དམ་གངས་ཅན་གྱི་རི" ("the king of mountains Meru, and Mount Mandara, and Mount Vindhya, or the mountain of snow [Himalaya]").

No claim states these readings are incompatible in the way `three-worlds.md`'s own recorded divergences are; they are recorded here as three independent attestations that the third mountain's name was not textually fixed across the tradition.

### Unique
`pema-namgyal:c-2-4-46` phrases the same shaking-verse gloss distinctively, describing Tārā's feet as stamping upon ground that is *likened to* Meru and the continents, rather than stating that she strikes Meru itself: "ཞབས་ནི་རིའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རི་རབ་དང་གླིང་རྣམས་ལྟ་བུའི་ས་ལ་བརྡབས་པས" ("her feet stamp upon the ground — [described as] like the king of mountains Meru together with the continents"). No other commentary in this packet frames the relationship between Meru and "the ground" struck as an explicit simile (ལྟ་བུ) in this way; elsewhere the mountain and the ground struck are treated as one and the same, not as compared to each other.

## A second, distinct verse: Meru struck by palm and stamped by foot, together with the earth and its continents

### Consensus
Eight commentaries gloss a separate root verse — not the Meru/Mandara/Vindhya-shaking verse above — in which Tārā, with a wrathful expression, strikes the earth's surface together with Mount Meru and its (four) continents with the palm of her hand and pounds it with her foot, an act several commentaries connect to crushing the seven subterranean levels with the syllable HŪM. `three-worlds.md` does not discuss this verse at all — it appears in this corpus's raw claims files under different node paths from the Meru/Mandara/Vindhya verse, at a different point in each commentary's own structure:

— attested: `anon-utpala:c-1-2-2-1-2-7-3`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-2-1-2-7-2`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-14-1-1`, `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-40`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-15-0-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-17-3`, `taranatha:c-14-3`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-14-3` (8 commentaries)

`anon-utpala:c-1-2-2-1-2-7-3` gives the fullest iconographic form: "རི་རབ་གླིང་དང་བཅས་པའི་ས་གཞི་ཆེན་པོའི་ངོས་ལ། སྡིགས་མཛུབ་དང་བཅས་པའི་ཕྱག་གི་མཐིལ་གྱིས་དྲག་ཏུ་བསྣུན་ཅིང་ཞབས་ཀྱི་མཐིལ་གྱིས་བརྡུང་བར་མཛད་མ" ("she strikes forcefully, with the threatening-gesture palm of her hand, the surface of the great earth together with Mount Meru and its continents, and pounds it with the sole of her foot"). `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-2-1-2-7-2`, `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-40`, and `sangye-nyentrul:c-15-0-1` restate the same act in near-identical wording. `taranatha:c-14-3` gives the terse form: "རི་རབ་གླིང་དང་བཅས་པའི་ས་གཞི་ཆེན་པོའི་ངོས་ལ། ཐལ་མོ་བརྡབས་པའི་ཕྱག་གི་མཐིལ་གིས་བསྣུན་པའོ" ("on the surface of the great earth, together with Mount Meru and the continents, she strikes with the palm of her clapping hand"). `palden-sherab:c-3-1-14-1-1` and `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-14-3` both extend the object struck to "Mount Meru together with the four continents" (རི་རབ་གླིང་བཞི་དང་བཅས་པ). `sungrab-tulku:c-17-3` gives the most expansive object: "རི་རབ་གླིང་བཞི་དང་གླིང་ཕྲན་བརྒྱད་བཅས་པའི་ས་གཞི་ཆེན་པོའི་ངོས" ("the surface of the great earth-base — Mount Meru, the four continents, and the eight sub-continents").

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed — every claim above treats this as one and the same act (palm-strike plus foot-stamp upon Meru and the earth), differing only in how many further cosmological elements (four continents, eight sub-continents) are named alongside Meru.

### Unique
None beyond the attestations above.

## Why name Meru specifically? — an argument from the mountain's own exceptional stability

### Consensus
None — this facet is attested by a single commentary's own grammatical-then-rhetorical argument.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`taranatha` alone poses and answers the question of why the root verse names Mount Meru by name at all, rather than leaving its movement implicit within "the three worlds" trembling. First, a grammatical note establishes that the shaking applies to all three named mountains together: `taranatha:c-17-9` — "འཇིག་རྟེན་གསུམ་ན་ཞེས་པ་གཉིས་པའི་དོན་ལ་བདུན་པའི་ཚིག་སྟེ་གསུམ་པོ་ལ་གཡོ་བའི་བྱེད་པ་འཇུག་པའོ" ("'in the three worlds' — the locative case used for the accusative sense — the action of shaking applies to [all] three"). The commentary then raises its own objection: `taranatha:c-17-10` — "འཇིག་རྟེན་གསུམ་སྐྱོད་པར་གྱུར་ན། རི་རབ་སོགས་སྨོས་པས་ཅི་ཞིག་བྱ་ཞེ་ན" ("if the three worlds are made to move, what purpose is served by mentioning Mount Meru and so forth?"). Its own answer names Meru's stability as exactly the point of naming it: `taranatha:c-17-11` — "སྤྱིར་འཇིག་རྟེན་གསུམ་སྐྱོད་པར་གྱུར་ཡང་། ལྷ་མཐུ་བོ་ཆ་དག་གནས་པའི་གནས་ཀྱི་ཁྱད་པར་དེ་རྣམས་གཡོ་བར་མི་འགྱུར་བ་ཡང་སྲིད་པས། འཕགས་མ་ཉིད་མཐུ་ཆེ་བའི་དོན་དུ་སྨོས་སོ" ("generally, even though the three worlds are made to move, it is possible that the special abodes where powerful gods reside might not move; therefore [Meru and so forth] are mentioned specifically to show the Noble Lady's great power"). In other words: Meru — as the abode of the world's most powerful gods (see "Identity," above, for `palden-sherab`'s matching epithet) — is precisely the kind of landmark whose stability might otherwise be assumed exempt from ordinary shaking; naming it by name, rather than trusting it to fall under "the three worlds" generally, is what proves the goddess's power is total.

## A rejected cosmological application: measuring the underworld's seven levels from Meru's summit

### Consensus
None — attested by a single commentary.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed — no other commentary in this packet takes a position on this specific question.

### Unique
`taranatha`, glossing the palm-and-foot-strike verse's mention of "the seven levels" crushed by the syllable HŪM (see "A second, distinct verse," above), reports and rejects an unnamed view that measures those levels from Mount Meru's own summit: `taranatha:c-14-8` — "ཁ་ཅིག་ནི་རི་རབ་ཀྱི་རྩེ་མོའི་ཁོངས་སེང་ནས་ཐུར་དུ་གཞལ་བ་ཡིན་ཟེར་ཡང་མི་ལེགས་པར་མངོན་ནོ" ("some say [these levels] are measured downward from within the summit of Mount Meru, but this appears incorrect"). No other commentary in this packet addresses this specific cosmological question about Meru's summit.

## Hidden, tantric meaning: Meru as the central channel

### Consensus
None as an independently corroborated statement — two commentaries give near-identical wording for a completion-stage reading of Meru specifically (distinct from `three-worlds.md`'s own Hidden/tantric readings facet, which covers the *three worlds'* — not Meru's own — channel-physiology mapping):

— attested: `taranatha:c-17-12`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-17-3-1` (2 commentaries)

`taranatha:c-17-12` states the identification in its shortest form: "སྦས་དོན་ནི། རི་རབ་ནི་ཚངས་པའི་རུས་པ" ("the hidden meaning: Mount Meru is the 'bone of Brahmā'"). `palden-sherab:c-3-1-17-3-1`, within a fuller four-part word/general/hidden/ultimate-meaning exegesis of the same verse, gives the identical identification with more anatomical detail: "རི་རབ་ནི་རྩ་དབུ་མ་ཚངས་པའི་རུས་པ་སྒལ་ཚིགས་སོ" ("'Meru' is the central channel, the spine, Brahmā's bone"). The near-verbatim match across these two commentaries suggests a shared exegetical source rather than fully independent corroboration — the same caution `2-RAILS/Claims/indra.md` and `2-RAILS/Claims/three-worlds.md` apply to comparably matched pairs elsewhere in this corpus. `palden-sherab:c-3-1-17-3-1` continues, beyond what is cited on `three-worlds.md`, to identify the two flanking mountains as the two side channels ("རི་གཞན་གཉིས་ནི་ཡིད་བཟང་མ་དང་དུང་ཅན་མ་ནི་གཡས་གཡོན་གྱི་རྩ་ཆེན་གཉིས") — a claim about Meru's companion peaks, not about Meru itself, so not further elaborated here.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
None beyond the corroborated pair above.

## Narrative: Meru churning the cosmic ocean, producing the sun and moon

### Consensus
None — attested by a single commentary.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`konchok-thabkhe`, glossing a verse in which Tārā holds a moon-marked maṇḍala (identified with her role as "deity of the [divine] lake"), reports a mahāsiddha's allusion to a Vedic myth in which Mount Meru itself churns the cosmic ocean: `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-18-4` — "གྲུབ་ཆེན་གྱིས། འཕགས་མ་ལྷ་ཡི་ངོ་འཛིན་ཞེས་གསུངས་པ་ནི། སྔོན་རི་རབ་ཀྱིས་བསྲུབས་ཤིང་བྱས་ཏེ་རྒྱ་མཚོ་བསྲུབས་པ་ལས་ཉི་ཟླ་གཉིས་ཐོན་པར་རིག་བྱེད་ཀྱི་གཞུང་ལས་འབྱུང་བ་དང་བསྟུན་པའོ" ("a mahāsiddha's statement that 'the Noble Lady is recognized as [the deity] of the lake' accords with the Vedic account that Mount Meru once churned the ocean, producing the sun and moon"). This is the only narrative role Meru is given anywhere in this packet outside the shaking/striking verses — no other commentary alludes to this or any comparable cosmogonic myth involving Meru.

### Unique

## Meru as a ritual measure and offering-substance

### Consensus
None — attested by a single commentary, in two separate ritual contexts within one work.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`tsultrim-namdak` alone uses Meru twice as a unit of scale within liturgical offering verses, outside any shaking or striking context. In the seven-branch offering's superior-offering verse: `tsultrim-namdak:c-5-2-4-2-2-1-1` — "ཕྱེ་མ་ཕུར་མ་རི་རབ་མཉམ་པ་དང" ("heaps of scented powder equal to Mount Meru," offered "to those victors"). And in the general maṇḍala offering to the Three Jewels: `tsultrim-namdak:c-5-2-5-1` — "གླིང་བཞིའི་རི་རབ་ཟླར་བཅས" ("the four continents with Mount Meru and the sun and moon," among the visualized universal wealth offered). In both cases Meru functions as the standard unit or centerpiece by which the offering's vastness is expressed, rather than as an object acted upon by Tārā. No other commentary in this packet uses Meru this way.

## Iconographic attribute: an emanation of Tārā holding Mount Meru

### Consensus
None — attested by a single commentary.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`tenga-tulku`, in its back-matter identification of the deity's twenty-member retinue, names Meru as the literal hand-implement of one specific emanation — the White Tārā who protects from the fear of wind: `tenga-tulku:c-z-6` — "རླུང་གི་འཇིགས་སྐྱོབ་སྒྲོལ་མ་དཀར་མོ། རི་རབ་འཛིན་པ" ("Tārā protecting from wind-fear, white, holding Mount Meru"). This is the only place in this packet where Meru appears as an iconographic attribute rather than as a cosmological object, a struck landmark, or a unit of measure.
