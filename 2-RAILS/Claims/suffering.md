## Questions asked

1. Does the corpus tie suffering explicitly to the framework of the four truths, and if so, where and how directly?
2. How do commentaries gloss the recurring "blissful, virtuous, peaceful[, her domain is nirvāṇa]" epithet-quartet, and does that gloss consistently key each term to the presence or absence of suffering?
3. Do any commentaries diverge on how this quartet should be read — as a simple description, or as a mapping onto the paths?
4. How is the name "Tārā" (sgrol ma) itself etymologically explained, and how often is that etymology keyed specifically to suffering rather than to fear, sin, or lower rebirth generally?
5. Is any one of the twenty-one homages named or titled by an epithet built on "suffering," and what does that epithet's exegesis contain?
6. Does any commentary give a typology of suffering (e.g. the three sufferings, six saṃsāric faults, eight sufferings) drawn from outside the root verses themselves?
7. Does any commentary connect suffering to the generation of renunciation (nges 'byung) as a distinct stage of the path, and how developed is that material?
8. Does the corpus attest a verse or petition asking the buddhas not to pass into nirvāṇa while beings remain "sunk in the ocean of suffering," and how many commentaries independently attest it?
9. What concrete forms of suffering (poverty, disease/epidemic, poison, lower rebirth, sin) does recitation or recollection of Tārā remove, according to the corpus, and how consistently is each attested?
10. Does any commentary supply narrative (hagiographic) illustrations of an individual's suffering being relieved through prayer to Tārā, distinct from the doctrinal or benefit-verse material?
11. Where does the term སྡུག་བསྔལ appear only incidentally — inside a stock phrase, or as backdrop to a different topic's exegesis — rather than as the claim's own analytical content, and how should those instances be treated?

## The "blissful/virtuous/peaceful[, nirvāṇa]" epithet-quartet as an implicit — and twice explicit — mapping onto the four truths

### Consensus

Ten of the sixteen commentaries, glossing the root verse's four epithets བདེ་མ (blissful), དགེ་མ (virtuous), ཞི་མ (peaceful), and "her domain is [the peace of] nirvāṇa," explain each term via the presence or absence of suffering and its causes — a structure that recurs closely enough across independent commentaries to read as a shared exegetical template keyed to result (suffering absent = blissful), cause (non-virtue/affliction abandoned = virtuous), path (affliction pacified by wisdom = peaceful), and cessation (nirvāṇa attained):

— attested: `anon-trinle-char:c-3-15-4`, `anon-utpala:c-1-2-2-2-3`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-80`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-81`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-2-2-2`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-15-1-1`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-16-0-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-18-4`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-2-2-3`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-15-3`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-50`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-51` (10 commentaries)

`anon-utpala:c-1-2-2-2-3` gives the minimal form: "འབྲས་བུ་སྡུག་བསྔལ་མེད་པས་བདེ་མ།" ("because her effect/result is free of suffering, she is 'blissful'"). `sungrab-tulku:c-18-4` gives the fullest four-part chain: "འབྲས་བུ་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཟད་པས་བདེ་མ། དེའི་རྒྱུ་ལས་ཉོན་གཉིས་སྤངས་པས་དགེ་མ། དེ་ཡང་ལམ་བདེན་སྟོང་ཉིད་རྟོགས་པའི་ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་བཅོམ་པས་ཞི་མ། ཉོན་སྒྲིབ་དང་ཤེས་སྒྲིབ་གཉིས་ཀྱི་བག་ཆགས་དང་བཅས་པ་མ་ལུས་པ་ཡོངས་སུ་སྤངས་པས་འགོག་པ་མྱ་ངན་ལས་འདས་ཤིང་ཞི་བ་ཆོས་སྐུ་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་སྤྱོད་ཡུལ་དང་ལྡན་པ་ནི་རྗེ་བཙུན་སྒྲོལ་མ་ཉིད་ལའོ།" — "because the resultant suffering is exhausted, she is 'blissful'; because its cause, the two afflictions, is abandoned, she is 'virtuous'; because this is accomplished by the wisdom realizing the truth of the path, she is 'peaceful'; because the propensities of both obscurations are thoroughly abandoned, she has passed beyond suffering, possessing the dharmakāya as her domain."

Two commentaries name the four-truths structure explicitly rather than leaving it implicit. `palden-sherab:c-3-1-15-1-1` states the correspondence directly: "འབྲས་བུ་སྡུག་བསྔལ་མེད་པས་བདེ་མ། རྒྱུ་ཀུན་འབྱུང་མི་དགེ་བ་སྤངས་པས་དགེ་མ། སྤང་བྱ་ལས་དང་ཉོན་མོངས་པ་དག ལམ་བདེན་བདག་མེད་རྟོགས་པའི་ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱིས་ཟད་པས་ཞི་མ། སྒྲིབ་གཉིས་ས་བོན་བག་ཆགས་དང་བཅས་པ་སྤངས་པས་འགོག་པ་མྱ་ངན་ལས་འདས་ཤིང་ཞི་བ་ཆོས་སྐུ་ཐོབ་པའོ།" — "'happy,' because the result, suffering, is absent; 'virtuous,' because the cause, non-virtue, is abandoned; 'peaceful,' because the objects to be abandoned — karma and afflictions — are exhausted by the wisdom realizing the truth of the path; 'nirvāṇa's peace,' because through abandoning the two obscurations the truth of cessation is attained." Independently, `tsultrim-namdak:c-9-2` — in an unrelated passage praising the Buddha's teaching — names the same four terms as the śrāvakayāna's own core doctrine: "སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཀུན་འབྱུང་འགོག་པ་ལམ" ("suffering, origin, cessation, path"), the corpus's clearest unprompted naming of the four truths as such (cited under "Typology of suffering," below, since its content is the four truths generally rather than this specific epithet-gloss).

### ⚑ Divergences

Gendun Drub's own commentary records two competing readings of this same epithet-quartet, presented as an internal tension rather than the commentary's single voice:

— `⚑ gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-2-3` — **Position 1** (attributed to "རྗེ་བཙུན་གྲགས་པ," i.e. Drakpa Gyaltsen): the four terms are simply undefiled bliss, freedom from afflictions, pacified suffering, and conceptuality-pacified nirvāṇa — "ཞི་མ་ནི། འབྲས་བུ་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཞི་མ། རྟོག་པ་ཞི་བའི་མྱ་ངན་ལས་འདས་པ་ལ་སྤྱོད་པ་ཉིད་མ་ཞེས་སོ།" **Position 2** (attributed to unnamed "མཁས་པ་ཁ་ཅིག," other scholars): the same four terms instead map onto the four paths — accumulation, preparation, seeing, and no-more-learning — with "peaceful" specifically explained as "མཐོང་ལམ་དུ་ས་བོན་དང་བྲལ་ནས། འབྲས་བུ་སྐྱེ་རྒ་ན་འཆི་སོགས་ཀྱི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཞི་བས་ཞི་མ།" — "on the path of seeing, having become free of the seeds, because the resultant suffering of birth, aging, sickness, and death is pacified, she is 'peaceful.'" This is the corpus's one explicit instance of divergent readings of the same suffering-keyed etymology, held within a single commentary rather than between two.

### Unique

None beyond the divergence recorded above.

## Etymology of "Tārā" (sgrol ma) — she who liberates beings from suffering

### Consensus

Six commentaries explain the deity's own name, སྒྲོལ་མ (Tārā, "liberator"), by directly deriving it from her liberating beings from suffering — distinct from, and in addition to, the "blissful/virtuous/peaceful" facet above, which explains a different set of epithets:

— attested: `anon-utpala:c-1-1-6`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-1-5`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-1-7`, `sungrab-tulku:c-3-6`, `sungrab-tulku:c-4-5`, `sungrab-tulku:c-26-3`, `palden-sherab:c-2-6`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-1-1-1`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-1-3` (6 commentaries)

The wording is close across independent commentaries. `anon-utpala:c-1-1-6`: "སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ལས་སྐྱོབ་པས་ན་སྒྲོལ་མ་སྟེ།" ("because she protects all sentient beings from suffering, she is 'Tārā'"). `lobsang-dawa:c-1-1-5`: "སེམས་ཅན་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ལས་སྐྱོབ་པས་ན་སྒྲོལ་མ་ལ་ཕྱག་འཚལ་ལོ" (near-identical). `gendun-drub:c-2-2-1-7`: "སྒྲོལ་མ་ནི། སྡུག་བསྔལ་གྱི་རྒྱ་མཚོ་ལས་སྒྲོལ་བའོ།" — she who liberates beings "from the ocean of suffering." `sungrab-tulku:c-3-6` gives the same core gloss ("སེམས་ཅན་འཁོར་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ལས་སྐྱོབ་པས་ནའོ།") twice more in the same file, at `sungrab-tulku:c-4-5` (karma, afflictions, and their resultant suffering) and `sungrab-tulku:c-26-3` (within a condensed etymology of OṂ Bhagavatī Ārya Tārā together). `palden-sherab:c-2-6`, citing Rongzompa, derives all three of "Jetsünma," "Phagma," and "Drolma" together, with Drolma glossed: "སེམས་ཅན་རྣམས་འཇིགས་དང་སྡུག་བསྔལ་རྒྱུ་འབྲས་ལས་སྐྱོབ་པས་ན་སྒྲོལ་མའོ།" ("because she protects beings from fear and from the causes and effects of suffering, she is Drolma"); `palden-sherab:c-3-1-1-1-1` repeats the same etymology in the commentary's own root-verse exegesis. `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-1-3` gives the same gloss a fourth independent time: "སྒྲོལ་མ་ཞེས་པ་སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ལས་སྐྱོབ་པ་དང་། ངན་སོང་ལས་སྐྱོབ་པའམ། འཁོར་བའི་འདམ་ལས་འདོན་པའི་ཆ་ནས་དེ་ལྟར་བཤད།"

### ⚑ Divergences

None observed. Every attestation converges on the same core derivation (protection/liberation from suffering), varying only in whether it is paired with protection from fear, from lower rebirth, or from saṃsāra's "mire" generally — differences of elaboration, not of doctrine.

### Unique

None beyond the attestations above.

## Epithet — the Lady Who Burns Away / Dispels All Suffering (nineteenth homage)

### Consensus

Four commentaries name or gloss the corpus's nineteenth homage by an epithet built directly on the word "suffering" — she who burns away, or dispels, all suffering — though the exact verb varies:

— attested: `anon-trinle-char:c-3-19-1`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-4-4`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-19-1`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-0-6` (4 commentaries)

`anon-trinle-char:c-3-19-1`: "ཕྱག་འཚལ་བཅུ་དགུ་པ་རྗེ་བཙུན་མ་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཐམས་ཅད་བསྲེག་པའི་སྒྲོལ་མ་" — "The Nineteenth Homage: to the Venerable Lady Who Burns Away All Suffering." `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-4-4` gives the same homage a parallel name: "འདི་ལ་སྡུག་བསྔལ་སྲེག་བྱེད་སྒྲོལ་མ་ཞེས་འབྱུང་ངོ་།" ("She is called here 'Tārā Who Burns Away Suffering'"). `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-19-1` uses a different verb for the same homage: "བཅུ་དགུ་པ་སྡུག་བསྔལ་སེལ་མ་ལ་བསྟོད་པ།" ("Nineteenth, homage to Dispeller of Suffering"). `tsultrim-namdak:c-0-6`, in an independent list of the deity's names, includes "སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཀུན་སེལ་མ" ("Dispeller of All Suffering") alongside other epithets. "Burns" (བསྲེག/སྲེག) and "dispels" (སེལ) name the same homage differently rather than disagreeing about its content.

### ⚑ Divergences

None observed.

### Unique

None beyond the attestations above.

## Typology of suffering — beyond the root verses' own vocabulary

### Consensus

None — the material below is contributed almost entirely by a single commentary (Tenzin Dhonzang), with one corroborating attestation each from two others; it is presented as Unique rather than Consensus.

### ⚑ Divergences

None observed.

### Unique

**Tenzin Dhonzang** supplies, by far, the corpus's most developed typology of suffering, none of it drawn from the root verses' own vocabulary but from external doctrinal and scriptural material embedded in the commentary's own exegesis:

- **The classical "three sufferings," "six faults," and "eight sufferings," enumerated together as objects of a purification visualization.** `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-12-4`: "སྡུག་བསྔལ་གྱི་སྡུག་བསྔལ། གྱུར་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ། ཁྱབ་པ་འདུ་བྱེད་ཀྱི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་གསུམ་... སྐྱེ་བའི་སྡུག་... འཆི་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ...ན་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ...རྒས་པའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ...དགྲ་མི་སྡུག་པ་དང་ཕྲད་པའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ། གཉེན་སྡུག་པ་དང་བྲལ་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ། འདོད་པ་བཙལ་ཀྱང་མི་རྙེད་པའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ། ཉེར་ལེན་གྱི་ཕུང་པོའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཏེ་བརྒྱད" — the three sufferings (of suffering, of change, and pervasive-conditioning), and the eight (birth, death, sickness, old age, meeting the unpleasant, separation from the pleasant, not obtaining what is sought, and the suffering of the appropriated aggregates); `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-15-8` repeats the same threefold/eightfold enumeration in an adjacent visualization. This is the corpus's only Abhidharma-style typology of suffering attested outside etymological glosses on the root verses.
- **A sutra quotation naming suffering as the mark of all contaminated phenomena, alongside impermanence and selflessness — the doctrinal "seals."** `tenzin-dhonzang:c-3-41`: "མདོ་ལས། འདུས་བྱས་ཐམས་ཅད་མི་རྟག་པ། ། ཟག་བཅས་ཐམས་ཅད་སྡུག་བསྔལ་བ། ། ཆོས་ཐམས་ཅད་སྟོང་ཞིང་བདག་མེད་པ།" ("From a sutra: 'All conditioned things are impermanent; all contaminated things are suffering; all phenomena are empty and selfless'"). No other commentary in the corpus quotes this or an equivalent formula.

Konchok Thabkhe independently corroborates suffering's structural role in the graded path, without the typology itself: `konchok-thabkhe:c-1-3-2` describes the pedagogical sequence of Buddhist guidance as beginning with a demonstration of "ངན་སོང་གི་སྡུག་བསྔལ" ("the suffering of the lower realms") to those with no thought for future lives, before graduated instruction through the three vehicles and four tantra classes — the same "suffering shown first" structure that underlies Tenzin Dhonzang's more extensive renunciation material (below), attested independently by a different commentary.

Tsultrim Namdak independently names the four truths themselves — see `tsultrim-namdak:c-9-2`, already quoted above under the epithet-quartet facet — as the core content of the śrāvakayāna, the corpus's only unprompted statement of the four truths as a formula, outside any gloss on the root verses.

## Suffering as the ground for generating renunciation (nges 'byung)

### Consensus

None — this material is unique to a single commentary.

### ⚑ Divergences

None observed.

### Unique

Tenzin Dhonzang alone develops an extended lam-rim-style excursus on contemplating saṃsāra's suffering as the method for generating renunciation, quoting several external texts not otherwise cited anywhere in the corpus:

- **The method for the "second renunciation":** `tenzin-dhonzang:c-3-20`: "ཕྱི་མའི་སྣང་ཤས་ལས་ལྡོག་པའི་ངེས་འབྱུང་བསྐྱེད་པའི་ཐབས་སུ་ལས་འབྲས་མི་བསླུ་བ་དང་། འཁོར་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཡང་ཡང་བསམ་པ་ལ་རྟེན་ནས།..." ("The method for generating the second renunciation is relying on repeated contemplation of the infallibility of karma and effect and saṃsāra's suffering...").
- **A quotation from the *Three Principal Aspects of the Path*:** `tenzin-dhonzang:c-3-22`: "ལས་འབྲས་མི་བསླུ་འཁོར་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་རྣམས། ། ཡང་ཡང་བསམས་པས་ཕྱི་མའི་སྣང་ཤས་ལྡོག།"
- **A quotation from the *Basis of All Good Qualities*:** `tenzin-dhonzang:c-3-26`: "སྤྱད་པས་མི་ངོམས་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཀུན་གྱི་སྒོ། ། ཡིད་བརྟན་མི་རུང་སྲིད་པའི་ཕུན་ཚོགས་ཀྱི།..." ("[Saṃsāra's excellence is] insatiable however enjoyed, the door to all suffering, and unreliable...").
- **The requirement of an uncontrived experience of suffering for genuine renunciation:** `tenzin-dhonzang:c-3-33`, arguing that a merely intellectual wish for freedom does not suffice — "ཁྱབ་པ་འདུ་བྱེད་ཀྱི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་འདི་ལ་སྐྱོ་བ་མ་སྐྱེས་ཀྱི་བར་འཁོར་བ་ལ་སྐྱོ་བ་རྣལ་མ་མི་འོང་བ།"
- **Two quotations from the "Melodious Laughter of Losang, Answers to Questions":** `tenzin-dhonzang:c-3-34` ("even animals, frightened by suffering upon suffering, wish for freedom") and `tenzin-dhonzang:c-3-35` (meditating on the aggregates as "a vessel accomplishing suffering, like a disease, its symptom, and a festering wound").
- **A closing doctrinal point that the afflictions are adventitious, not intrinsic to mind, which is precisely what makes the exhaustion of suffering possible:** `tenzin-dhonzang:c-3-70`: "ཉོན་མོངས་རྣམས་ནི་གློ་བུར་བའམ་འཕྲལ་གྱི་རྣམ་རྟོག་ཙམ་ལས་སེམས་ཀྱི་རང་གཤིས་སུ་མ་ཞུགས་པས། ...སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཟད་རུང་། འགོག་བདེན་ཐོབ་རུང་ཡིན་པ་སྟེ།"

No other commentary in the corpus develops this argument or cites any of these four external texts (*Three Principal Aspects of the Path*, *Basis of All Good Qualities*, *Melodious Laughter of Losang*, and the unnamed sutra already cited above under "Typology of suffering").

## The bodhisattva's vow not to pass into nirvāṇa while beings drown in the "ocean of suffering"

### Consensus

Two commentaries independently quote what is close to the same verse — a petition asking the buddhas, bodhisattvas, and gurus not to pass into nirvāṇa for as long as saṃsāra is not emptied, but to look with compassion on beings sunk in the "ocean of suffering":

— attested: `pema-namgyal:c-1-6-2`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-5-2-1-6-1` (2 commentaries)

`pema-namgyal:c-1-6-2`: "འཁོར་བ་ཇི་སྲིད་མ་སྟོངས་བར། ། མྱ་ངན་མི་འདའ་ཐུགས་རྗེ་ཡིས། ། སྡུག་བསྔལ་རྒྱ་མཚོར་བྱིང་བ་ཡི། ། སེམས་ཅན་རྣམས་ལ་གཟིགས་སུ་གསོལ།" `tsultrim-namdak:c-5-2-1-6-1` quotes the identical four lines verbatim within its own "requesting non-passing" ritual branch, then glosses it: "བདག་སོགས་འཁོར་བ་སྡུག་བསྔལ་གྱི་རྒྱ་མཚོར་བྱིང་ཞིང་སྡུག་བསྔལ་གསུམ་གྱིས་མནར་བའི་སེམས་ཅན་རྣམས་ལ་གཟིགས་ནས་མངོན་མཐོ་དང་ངེས་ལེགས་ཀྱི་གོ་འཕང་ལ་དགོད་དུ་གསོལ།" — extending the request to establish beings, "tormented by the three sufferings," in higher rebirth and definitive excellence. The verbatim match across two otherwise unrelated commentaries (one unaffiliated, one Kagyu) suggests a shared liturgical source rather than independent composition, though neither claim names one.

### ⚑ Divergences

None observed.

### Unique

None beyond the corroborated pair above.

## Protection from suffering — the central practical benefit of recitation, recollection, and mantra

### Consensus

Fifteen of the sixteen commentaries (all but Lobsang Dawa, whose two claims on this topic fall under the etymology facets above) attest, in at least one claim, that reciting the homages, recollecting Tārā's form, or invoking her mantra removes some concrete form of suffering for the practitioner and often for others. The claims cluster into four recurring, specific benefit-statements rather than one undifferentiated claim:

**Statement 1 — protection from the suffering of poverty and destitution**, glossing the verse on Tārā liberating the destitute:

— attested: `anon-utpala:c-1-2-2-1-2-4-5`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-67`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-11-1-2`, `sungrab-tulku:c-14-4`, `taranatha:c-11-6`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-11-4`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-11-6`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-40` (7 commentaries)

`taranatha:c-11-6`: "འགྲོ་བ་ཕོངས་བ་རྣམས་ཐམས་ཅད་དབུལ་ཞིང་ཕོངས་པའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་མ་ལུས་པ་ལས་སྒྲོལ་ཞིང་ཞི་བར་མཛད་པ།" ("she liberates and pacifies all destitute beings from every suffering of poverty and want"). `sungrab-tulku:c-14-4` and `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-11-4` give near-identical wordings tying this to the wrathful HŪṂ-light iconography of the eleventh homage.

**Statement 2 — protection from the suffering caused by spirits, epidemic disease, and poison**, glossing the closing "benefits for this life" verse:

— attested: `gendun-gyatso:c-2-5`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-3-3-4`, `karma-maitri:c-1-2-13`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-3-6`, `palden-sherab:c-3-2-2-4-1`, `pema-namgyal:c-3-3`, `sungrab-tulku:c-23-4`, `taranatha:c-22-1-33`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-3-3-11`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-3-3-12`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-3-3-14`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-5-2-7-4-3-1`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-5-2-7-4-3-1-1` (10 commentaries)

`tenga-tulku:c-1-3-3-11` quotes the root verse itself: "གདོན་དང་རིམས་ནད་དུག་གིས་གཟིར་བའི། ། … སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཚོགས་ནི་རྣམ་པར་སྤང་སྟེ།" ("Afflicted by evil spirits, epidemic disease, and poison, the mass of suffering is entirely abandoned"). `konchok-thabkhe:c-3-6` and `gendun-drub:c-2-2-3-3-4` gloss the same verse independently, in near-identical wording. `gendun-gyatso:c-2-5` extends the same pattern to "harm from possessing spirits" and "internal/external poison," explicitly noting the benefit extends to others as well as oneself; `palden-sherab:c-3-2-2-4-1` and `pema-namgyal:c-3-3` give the same closing-verse gloss independently, and `tenga-tulku:c-1-3-3-14` extends it explicitly to protecting other beings, not only the reciter.

**Statement 3 — protection from the suffering of the lower realms and of saṃsāra generally, as the ultimate (rather than merely temporary) benefit of practice**:

— attested: `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-2-13`, `gendun-gyatso:c-2-3`, `karma-maitri:c-1-2-7`, `palden-sherab:c-3-2-2-1-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-2-9`, `sungrab-tulku:c-18-5`, `taranatha:c-20-3`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-2-1-2-1-4`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-8-1-4` (8 commentaries)

`sungrab-tulku:c-2-9` gives the clearest temporal/ultimate framing: "གནས་སྐབས་ནི། འཇིགས་པ་བརྒྱད་དང་བཅུ་དྲུག་ལ་སྐྱབས་པར་མཛད་པ་དང་། མཐར་ཐུག་ནི། ...བྱེ་བྲག་ཏུ་ངན་སོང་གི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཐམས་ཅད་འཇོམས་པ་ཉིད་དོ།" ("temporarily, she protects from the eight and sixteen fears; ultimately... she specifically destroys all the suffering of the lower realms").

**Statement 4 — mantra recitation or recollection destroys the sin/misdeed that is suffering's cause, and thereby its resultant suffering**:

— attested: `anon-utpala:c-1-2-2-2-9`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-1-2-4-3`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-11-5`, `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-43`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-0-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-16-0-2`, `taranatha:c-15-8`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-9-3`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-10-5`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-3`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-5-3-3` (8 commentaries)

`sangye-nyentrul:c-0-2`, from the commentary's own opening verse, gives the strongest single-clause form of this statement: "ཁྱེད་ཞབས་དྲན་ཙམ་གྱིས་ཀྱང་དུཿཁའི་འགྲོ། ། སྡུག་བསྔལ་རྒྱུ་བཅས་མིང་གི་ལྷག་མ་རུ། ། བགྱིད་མཛད་..." ("merely recollecting your feet reduces the suffering realms, together with their causes, to a mere name").

### ⚑ Divergences

None observed. No commentary denies or qualifies the general pattern that recitation, recollection, or mantra removes suffering; commentaries differ only in which specific form of suffering they choose to illustrate, which is a difference of selection, not of doctrine.

### Unique

None beyond the attestations above; the pattern is corroborated across nearly the entire corpus.

## Narrative illustrations of protection from specific sufferings

### Consensus

None — this material is unique to a single commentary.

### ⚑ Divergences

None observed.

### Unique

Tsultrim Namdak alone supplies a set of hagiographic narratives, each explicitly framed as an illustration of Tārā's protection from a *named* category of suffering, distinct in kind from the doctrinal and benefit-verse material found elsewhere in the corpus:

- **Protection from imprisonment/suffering under royal punishment.** `tsultrim-namdak:c-4-7-1`: a chief of thieves, caught drunk while robbing the king's treasury, is imprisoned and tortured — "ཆད་པ་སྣ་ཚོགས་པ་གཅད་ནས་སྡུག་བསྔལ་བའི་ཚེ་སྒྲོལ་མ་ལ་གསོལ་བ་བཏབས་པས" ("when he prayed to Tārā while suffering various punishments") — until a multicolored bird unties his bonds and the prison door swings open.
- **Protection from the suffering of poverty.** `tsultrim-namdak:c-4-12-1`: a poor farmer, "ཟས་གོས་ཀྱིས་ཕོངས་ནས་སྡུག་བསྔལ་བའི་ཚེ" ("destitute of food and clothing and suffering"), prays to Tārā and is led by a prophetic vision and a bell-adorned horse's tracks to buried gold and silver.
- **Protection from the suffering of separation from loved ones.** `tsultrim-namdak:c-4-13-1`: a wealthy brahmin loses his entire family to an epidemic — the section is headed "གཉེན་དང་བྲལ་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ལས་བསྐྱབས་པ" ("protection from the suffering of separation from loved ones") — and, after a pilgrimage to a Tārā festival in Vārāṇasī, obtains a new family.
- **Deliverance from mortal suffering at sea.** `tsultrim-namdak:c-4-28-1`: a shipwrecked merchant, drowning, prays to Tārā; her emanation as a white turtle addresses him directly — "སྡུག་བསྔལ་གྱི་མནར་བའི་སེམས་ཅན་ཁྱེད་ངས་བསྐྱབ" ("being tormented by suffering, I shall save you") — and carries him to shore.

No other commentary in the corpus supplies comparable individually-named narrative illustrations tied to a specific suffering-category; the pattern elsewhere in the corpus is doctrinal/etymological (facets above) or verse-exegetical (Statements 1–4 above), not hagiographic.
