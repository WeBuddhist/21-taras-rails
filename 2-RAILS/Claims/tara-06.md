## Structural placement

### Consensus
Eight commentaries mark this passage with an explicit flat ordinal — "the sixth" (དྲུག་པ) or "(6)" — identifying it as the sixth of the twenty-one homages.

— attested: `yama-sonam:c-3-6-1`, `gendun-gyatso:c-1-6-1`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-1`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-6-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-0-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-9-1`, `taranatha:c-6-1`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-1` (8 commentaries)

`palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-0-1` and `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-1` are also cited under "Naming and epithets" below, for the epithets the same claims supply alongside the ordinal; both statements are genuinely present in their text.

### ⚑ Divergences
Four commentaries instead nest this homage within a different classificatory scheme, labelling it "the fifth" (ལྔ་པ) rather than using the flat ordinal — the same four commentaries, at the same shared node path (1.2.2.1.1.5 / 2.2.2.2.1.1.5), that diverged identically at verse 4 in `2-RAILS/Claims/tara-04.md`. Their own openers frame this as the fifth *mode of praise* (praise "through being honoured by the great worldly gods," or "by way of the great world-protectors' reverence"), not a renumbering of which of the twenty-one homages this is.

- Position 1 (flat "sixth of twenty-one"): the eight commentaries listed under Consensus above.
- Position 2 (nested "fifth mode of praise"): `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-1`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-1-1-5-1`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-1`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-1`.

This is a difference in classificatory axis, not a doctrinal disagreement about the verse's content — recorded here per Rule 4 rather than folded into the Consensus, since these four commentaries' own structural claims do not label the verse "the sixth" anywhere captured in the packet.

### Unique
None beyond the two positions above.

## Root verse quoted

### Consensus
Seven commentaries quote the root verse's full four lines together in one block (subject to minor orthographic variation across transmissions): "ཕྱག་འཚལ་བརྒྱ་བྱིན་མེ་ལྷ་ཚངས་པ། ། རླུང་ལྷ་སྣ་ཚོགས་དབང་ཕྱུག་མཆོད་མ། ། འབྱུང་པོ་རོ་ལངས་དྲི་ཟ་རྣམས་དང་། ། གནོད་སྦྱིན་ཚོགས་ཀྱིས་མདུན་ནས་བསྟོད་མ། །" — "Homage to her who is worshipped by Indra, Agni, Brahmā, Vāyu, and Sarveśvara, and praised from in front by hosts of bhūtas, vetālas, gandharvas, and yakṣas."

— attested: `yama-sonam:c-3-6-3`, `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-2`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-0-2`, `sungrab-tulku:c-9-2`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-2`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-2`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-19` (7 commentaries)

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed on the verse's wording itself — the variation is orthographic only.

### Unique
Anon-Trinle-Char's quotation is uniquely prefaced "from the tantra" (རྒྱུད་ལས།) rather than presented as the praise's own verse — the same distinctive attribution this commentary gives at verse 4. `yama-sonam:c-3-6-3` (already cited above; noted here for the attribution)

Tenga Tulku's quotation preserves internal ellipsis marks between each line in the packet's own text (copied verbatim, not introduced by this page).

Drakpa Gyaltsen quotes only the verse's first two lines ("ཕྱག་འཚལ་བརྒྱ་བྱིན་མེ་ལྷ་ཚངས་པ། ། རླུང་ལྷ་སྣ་ཚོགས་དབང་ཕྱུག་མཆོད་མ། །"), not the full four lines, as the opening of a word-by-word gloss; per Rule 9 this is a partial attestation, not folded into the full-quotation count above. `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-34`

Lobsang Dawa splits the verse across two separately-numbered fragments, following the same distinct quatrain-line verse-numbering convention already observed at verse 4: lines 1–2 as "Verse 14" (`lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-2`) and lines 3–4 as "Verse 15" (`lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-3`).

Gendun Drub does not quote the verse's exact wording at all; its opening claim is a paraphrase substituting exegetical language ("to whom," "and so forth," "chief ones") for the verse's own words, and is therefore cited under "The four principal worshipping gods," below, rather than here. `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-1-1-5-2`

## Naming and epithets

### Consensus
None at the level of a single shared proper name — see Divergences.

### ⚑ Divergences
Three distinct naming traditions are attested for this particular Tārā:

**Position 1 — "[Tārā] Victorious over the Three Worlds" (འཇིག་རྟེན་གསུམ་ལས་རྣམ་པར་རྒྱལ་བ):** Anon-Trinle-Char's section heading and Tenzin Dhonzang's heading give this exact epithet in verbatim-identical wording (re-read and confirmed: both read འཇིག་རྟེན་གསུམ་ལས་རྣམ་པར་རྒྱལ་བ character-for-character), independently naming her the same way.

— attested: `yama-sonam:c-3-6-1`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-1` (2 commentaries, verbatim-identical epithet)

**Position 2 — "Tārā Utterly Victorious" (རྣམ་པར་རྒྱལ་བའི་སྒྲོལ་མ), related but not identical:** Gendun Drub gives this shorter epithet, sharing the "rnam par rgyal ba" (victorious) root with Position 1 but omitting the "over the three worlds" qualifier (re-read and confirmed: the two phrases are not the same compound, so this is recorded as a separate, related position rather than merged into Position 1).

— attested: `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-1-1-5-4` (1 commentary)

**Position 3 — "Great Terrifier" ('jigs byed chen mo), frequently paired with "Destroyer of the Spirit-Classes" (gdon rigs rnam par 'joms pa / gdon rigs rnams 'joms ma):** Four commentaries independently give this epithet. Palden Sherab's and Tsultrim Namdak's formulations share the same core compound (གདོན་རིགས་...འཇོམས་) but differ grammatically — Palden Sherab uses a verbal construction (གདོན་རིགས་རྣམ་པར་འཇོམས་པ, "utterly destroying the spirit-classes"), Tsultrim Namdak a nominal/agentive one (གདོན་རིགས་རྣམས་འཇོམས་མ, "destroyer of the spirit-classes") — re-read and confirmed as a genuine grammatical difference, not a copying error. This is an independent corroboration of the same underlying two-part name, though not verbatim-identical. Sungrab Tulku gives the bare "Tārā the Great Terrifier" without the "destroyer" qualifier. Sangye Nyentrul gives a related but distinct epithet using the near-homophonous verb "gathers" (འཛོམས་པ) rather than "destroys" (འཇོམས་པ) — "Tārā, Great Terrifier, Who *Gathers* the Hosts of Obstructing Spirits" — a genuinely different meaning from the other three despite the visual/aural similarity, so it is recorded as a distinct sub-variant within Position 3 rather than folded into the pair above.

— attested (shared core compound "Destroyer of the Spirit-Classes, Great Terrifier," grammatical variants — verbal vs. nominal): `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-0-1`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-3-9` (2 commentaries)

— attested (bare "Great Terrifier," no qualifier): `sungrab-tulku:c-9-3` (1 commentary)

— attested ("Great Terrifier Who *Gathers* the Hosts," distinct verb): `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-1-1` (1 commentary)

Four commentaries in total attest Position 3, with the "gathers/destroys" distinction noted above.

### Unique
None beyond the three positions above. The remaining nine commentaries either give no proper epithet for this homage in the claims captured, or use only the generic "Tārā" / "the Venerable Lady."

## The four principal worshipping gods

### Consensus
The verse's first two lines name Indra, Agni, Brahmā, and Vāyu among the worshippers. Five commentaries additionally agree on a clean four-point directional-guardian assignment for these four: Indra guarding the east, Agni the southeast, Brahmā the zenith/above, and Vāyu the northwest.

— attested (directional 4-guardian assignment, E/SE/above/NW): `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-3`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-2`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-6-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-0-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-9-5` (5 commentaries)

Tenga Tulku's own claim offers this same four-point directional assignment explicitly as an *alternative* reading ("ཡང་ན," "or alternatively") alongside a first, non-directional reading (Indra as "lord of gods," Brahmā as "master of the trichiliocosm") — it is therefore not folded into the clean 5-commentary count above, since the claim itself does not commit to the directional reading as its primary gloss. `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-3`

### ⚑ Divergences
None on the identity of the four gods themselves — no commentary disputes that Indra, Agni, Brahmā, and Vāyu are meant. The divergence over the *fifth* worshipper, Sarveśvara/Viśveśvara, is recorded separately below.

### Unique
Several commentaries characterize the four gods by function or epithet rather than (or in addition to) direction. Taranatha's word-glosses are the fullest of this type: Indra is "king of the class of gods," Agni is "chief of the sages," Brahmā is "held to be the creator of the world," and Vāyu "effects the particulars of the world's formation and arrangement." `taranatha:c-6-3`, `taranatha:c-6-4`, `taranatha:c-6-5`, `taranatha:c-6-6`

Tsultrim Namdak's own glosses for Agni ("chief among sages") and Brahmā ("held to be the creator of the world") are near-verbatim matches to Taranatha's wording above (re-read and confirmed: "དྲང་སྲོང་གི་གཙོ་བོ་མེ་ལྷ" / "མེ་ལྷ་ནི་དྲང་སྲོང་གི་གཙོ་བོའོ" for Agni, and "འཇིག་རྟེན་བྱེད་པོ་འདོད་པ་ཚངས་པ" / "ཚངས་པ་འཇིག་རྟེན་གྱི་བྱེད་པོར་འདོད་པའོ" for Brahmā) — a third instance in this corpus (alongside the elemental-correspondence match recorded under "Esoteric interpretation," below) of these two commentaries sharing near-identical wording, consistent with a shared exegetical source. `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-19`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-20`

Drakpa Gyaltsen and Karma Maitri each give the four gods as four separate atomized single-word glosses, naming each in turn without elaboration or direction. `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-34`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-35`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-36`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-37`; `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-2`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-3`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-4`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-5`

Pema Namgyal gives a distinctive elaborated set of epithets not found elsewhere in the corpus: Indra as "great among the Thirty-Three," Agni as "great among sages," Brahmā as "great in the form realm," and Vāyu as "able to move the three states of existence." `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-24`

Three commentaries state, as a shared activity, that even these greatest worldly gods become objects of her worship: Drakpa Gyaltsen ("all five gods offer worship at her feet"), Karma Maitri ("these great lords are said to worship her"), and Taranatha ("even those most renowned in the world become objects of worship [to her]").

— attested: `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-39`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-7`, `taranatha:c-6-8` (3 commentaries)

Gendun Gyatso and Gendun Drub each give a paraphrase-style identification of the four gods (plus Sarveśvara) as "the great lords... great gods of the world" without directional assignment. `gendun-gyatso:c-1-6-1`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-1-1-5-2`

Tenzin Dhonzang's own restatement names Vāyu and "the great mundane gods of manifold forms such as Maheśvara" as worshippers, without directions and without distinguishing Sarveśvara as a separate figure from this list. `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-4`

Tsultrim Namdak's opening claim additionally names Indra as the first worshipper, folded directly into its full verse-quotation (cited above under "Root verse quoted"). `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-19` (already cited above)

## Identity of Sarveśvara / Viśveśvara ("the various lord")

### Consensus
None — see Divergences. Sungrab Tulku and the five-commentary directional-scheme group cited above under "The four principal worshipping gods" name "Sarveśvara" as the fifth worshipper without further identifying who this is: `sungrab-tulku:c-9-5` names him as the southern-quarter figure with no gloss beyond the name itself.

### ⚑ Divergences
At least four distinct identifications of this figure are attested, none reconciled with any other in the corpus:

**Position 1 — Maheśvara ("the Great Wrathful Lord," དབང་ཕྱུག་ཆེན་པོ):** Drakpa Gyaltsen identifies Maheśvara explicitly as "lord of these [preceding] four gods," who "offers worship at her feet."

— attested: `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-38` (1 commentary)

**Position 2 — Yama (south):** Sangye Nyentrul's directional list identifies the "many-formed lord" (སྣ་ཚོགས་དབང་ཕྱུག) explicitly as Yama, guardian of the south.

— attested: `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-0-1` (1 commentary)

**Position 3 — the water-god (west) and the earth-goddess Tenma, jointly:** Konchok Thabkhe identifies "Various Lord" as *two* deities rather than one, with explicit reasoning: ritual manuals state one faces west during power-rites because the western water-god holds the power-drawing noose, and the tantra class states the earth-goddess Tenma has power over the four continents — hence the epithet applies to both. (The claim itself states only that Tenma "has power over the four continents," with no direction given; other commentaries in this corpus place the earth-goddess at the nadir/below, e.g. under "Extended directional-guardian schemes," but this claim does not itself state a direction for her.)

— attested: `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-6-2`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-6-5` (1 commentary, 2 claims)

**Position 4 — a class of "variously renowned" lords (Rudra, Kāma, the sun-coloured sage, Bhaga, and others), not one single deity:** Taranatha glosses "Sarveśvara" (སྣ་ཚོགས་དབང་ཕྱུག) as a group-name for multiple gods "variously renowned as creators or lords." Tsultrim Namdak's own gloss is related but not identical — "the fierce ones, and the various lords such as those wishing to belong [to her retinue]" — naming no specific deities, so it is recorded as a related but distinct sub-attestation rather than merged with Taranatha's fuller list. Karma Maitri's bare "that is, the great lords [plural]" (དབང་ཕྱུག་ཆེན་པོ་དག) is consistent with this same "class, not one deity" framing, but likewise without naming individual gods.

— attested (named cluster: Rudra, Kāma, sun-coloured sage, Bhaga): `taranatha:c-6-7` (1 commentary)
— attested (unnamed plural "various lords," consistent with Position 4 but narrower): `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-21`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-6` (2 further commentaries)

### Unique
None beyond the four positions above.

## Extended directional-guardian schemes

### Consensus
None shared identically by all contributing commentaries — see Divergences. Several commentaries independently extend the verse's four or five worshippers into a fuller scheme of eight, ten, or fifteen directional guardians.

### ⚑ Divergences
Konchok Thabkhe's own commentary flags this extension explicitly as an innovation, not a received reading: "although no [earlier] commentary connects this verse with the directional guardians, most of their names appear directly in the tantra's own words," citing further that "the Jowo" (ཇོ་བོ — identity unspecified in the raw claim, `Referent: [unanchored]`), in his own liturgical compilation, states that the mantra-garland's light subdues the ten directional guardians, and that this mahāsiddha (the author) has several sādhanas of his own showing the venerable lady surrounded by directional guardians. `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-6-4`

Given this explicit flag, the following expanded schemes are recorded as independent commentarial extensions rather than a single received tradition:

**A ten-directional-guardian cluster (Yama–Varuṇa–Sthāvarā–Viṣṇu–Maheśvara added to the core four):** Palden Sherab's elaborate list continues the four principal gods with Yama (south), Varuṇa "lord of nāgas" (west), the earth-goddess Sthāvarā (below), Viṣṇu as "great-handed holder of the earth," and Maheśvara as a further world-creator. Sangye Nyentrul's own list independently adds the same three figures in near-identical, in places verbatim, phrasing for the water/nāga lord (west), the earth-goddess Brtan-ma (below), and "earth-holder, great hand" (ས་འཛིན་ལག་པ་ཆེན་པོ — verbatim identical to Palden Sherab's phrase; re-read and confirmed as genuine correspondence, not coincidence).

— attested: `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-1-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-1-3`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-0-1` (2 commentaries; `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-0-1` already cited above under "The four principal worshipping gods" and "Identity of Sarveśvara," for the same claim's further statements)

Tenzin Dhonzang independently arrives at "the ten guardians of the directions" by a different route: starting from an eight-guardian scheme cited from an external liturgical source (below), then adding Brahmā (zenith) and the earth-goddess Tenma (nadir) to reach ten. `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-7`

Anon-Trinle-Char's own reasoning invokes "the ten directional guardians" by name when identifying Agni and Vāyu, without enumerating the full set. `yama-sonam:c-3-6-5`

Pema Namgyal's elaborated identification likewise names "the ten guardians of the directions" alongside its own epithet-list for the four principal gods. `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-24`

**An eight-directional-guardian scheme, cited from an external liturgical source:** Tenzin Dhonzang alone cites a specific page of a named liturgical text (notes on Cakrasaṃvara's subtle-drop-body maṇḍala) assigning: Indra (east), Yama (south), the water-god (west), Vaiśravaṇa (north), Agni (southeast), a rākṣasa (southwest), the wind-god (northwest), and Īśāna [= Maheśvara] (northeast). `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-6`

**A fifteen-directional-guardian scheme:** Tenzin Dhonzang alone extends its own ten-guardian total (above) further, adding the sun and moon, "Sudṛṣṭimaṇḍala," Gaṇapati, and Nyewang (identified with Viṣṇu), reaching fifteen — stated to be identical to "the fifteen guardians of the directions in the context of Bhairava." `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-7` (already cited above for the ten-guardian portion of the same claim)

**Additional worshippers named beyond the directional-guardian frame:** Anon-Utpala separately names Yama, nāgas, and "various-class lords" as exemplified by the four principal gods, worshipping her as their chief object of reverence. Konchok Thabkhe separately names "the powerful elemental spirit" (dbang ldan), "the corpse-riding rākṣasa," and — distinctively — "Yama, lord of the gandharvas" (a role elsewhere in this corpus assigned to Indra; see "Divergences" under the retinue-classes facet below), together with the yakṣa-hosts guarding the north.

— attested: `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-4`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-6-3` (2 commentaries)

### Unique
None beyond the schemes above.

## The four retinue-classes (bhūta, vetāla, gandharva, yakṣa)

### Consensus
Multiple commentaries agree the verse's third and fourth lines name four classes of spirit-beings — bhūta, vetāla, gandharva, and yakṣa — who praise her from in front, and that even these normally hard-to-tame, fear-inspiring hosts do so with reverence. The bare fact of this fourfold praise (without exemplar-naming) is independently stated by:

— attested: `yama-sonam:c-3-6-6`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-13`, `taranatha:c-6-14` (3 commentaries)

Several commentaries go further and frame each class as the specific retinue of one of the principal worshipping gods ("bhūta, the retinue of X," etc.), though they do not agree on which god each class serves — see Divergences.

### ⚑ Divergences

**Who exemplifies/rules the vetāla class?**
- Position A (Maheśvara): Drakpa Gyaltsen ("vetālas means [beings] such as Maheśvara"), Gendun Drub ("the vetāla chief, Maheśvara" — verbatim-matching Drakpa Gyaltsen's exemplar-set for three of the four classes, re-read and confirmed as a genuine three-way correspondence, not topical overlap: both independently pair bhūta↔Gaṇapati, vetāla↔Maheśvara, gandharva↔the five-crested ones), and Tsultrim Namdak ("the vetālas [are ruled by] Maheśvara and others"). The fourth pairing (yakṣa) is related but not identical between Drakpa Gyaltsen and Gendun Drub — see "Who exemplifies the yakṣa class?" below, where the two are recorded as distinct sub-positions.
- Position B (an unnamed vetāla "dwelling in the charnel ground," no Maheśvara identification): Karma Maitri and Gendun Gyatso, who instead pair vetāla with a charnel-ground dwelling-place rather than a ruling deity.
- Position C ("Legden," a specifically named rākṣasa of the southwest, distinct from either of the above): Palden Sherab and Sangye Nyentrul, whose retinue-lists independently place a rākṣasa named Legden (ལེགས་ལྡན) in the vetāla/southwest slot.

— attested (Position A): `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-41`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-1-1-5-3`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-22` (3 commentaries)

— attested (Position B): `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-10`, `gendun-gyatso:c-1-6-2` (2 commentaries)

— attested (Position C): `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-1-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-0-2` (2 commentaries)

**Who exemplifies the yakṣa class?**
- Position A1 (Vaiśravaṇa himself, རྣམ་ཐོས་སྲས): Drakpa Gyaltsen, Taranatha (རྣམ་ཐོས་བུ, glossed "Vaiśravaṇa and other lords of treasure"), Tenzin Dhonzang (Chakrasaṃvara citation, cited above), Palden Sherab, Sangye Nyentrul, Sungrab Tulku (implicit, north-guardian role), and Tsultrim Namdak.
- Position A2 (the son of Vaiśravaṇa, རྣམ་ཐོས་ཀྱི་སྲས — related but a distinct figure by this raw file's own English gloss): Gendun Drub alone. Its claim's explicit genitive construction ("ཀྱི་སྲས," translated in that raw file's own English as "the son of Vaiśravaṇa") distinguishes it from Position A1's naming of Vaiśravaṇa himself, so it is recorded as a separate, related sub-position rather than merged into that pairing (re-read and confirmed: the difference is in the source claim's own wording, not introduced by this page).
- Position B ("Smug-'dzin" / "Rmugs-'dzin," a differently-named figure): Karma Maitri and Gendun Gyatso — the same two commentaries that independently diverge on the vetāla exemplar above, again pairing with each other rather than with the Vaiśravaṇa majority (re-read and confirmed: "སྨུག་འཛིན" and "རྨུགས་འཛིན" are near-homophonous spellings of what appears to be the same figure-name).

— attested (Position A1): `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-43`, `taranatha:c-6-12`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-1-4`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-0-2`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-22` (5 commentaries)

— attested (Position A2): `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-1-1-5-3` (1 commentary)

Tenzin Dhonzang's Chakrasaṃvara citation and Sungrab Tulku's north-guardian retinue claim (both already cited above, under "Extended directional-guardian schemes" and "The four retinue-classes" respectively) are consistent with Position A1's Vaiśravaṇa identification but do not independently name Vaiśravaṇa within the specific retinue-role claim itself, so they are not added to the count above.

— attested (Position B): `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-12`, `gendun-gyatso:c-1-6-2` (2 commentaries)

**Which god does each retinue serve — a further, independent axis of disagreement:** Four commentaries frame the classes explicitly as retinues ("'khor") of named gods, but disagree on the assignments. Anon-Utpala: bhūta = retinue of "the powerful one" (dbang ldan), vetāla = retinue of rākṣasas, gandharva = retinue of Indra, yakṣa = retinue of "the ill-bodied one." Lobsang Dawa: bhūta = retinue of *Viṣṇu* (dbang ldan), vetāla = retinue of rākṣasas, gandharva = retinue of Indra, yakṣa = retinue of "the ill-formed." Sungrab Tulku: bhūta = retinue of Īśvara (dbang ldan), vetāla = retinue of rākṣasas, gandharva = retinue of Indra, yakṣa = retinue of "the evil-bodied ones." These three converge closely (dbang ldan → bhūta; rākṣasas → vetāla; Indra → gandharva) but differ on precisely who "dbang ldan" is (unnamed "powerful one" vs. Viṣṇu vs. Īśvara — re-read and confirmed these are not the same gloss). Konchok Thabkhe departs further, identifying Yama — not Indra — as "lord of the gandharvas."

— attested (dbang-ldan/rākṣasa/Indra/ill-bodied retinue pattern, three variant namings of "dbang ldan"): `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-5`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-3`, `sungrab-tulku:c-9-7` (3 commentaries)

Konchok Thabkhe's own claim (already cited above under "Extended directional-guardian schemes") departs from this pattern, identifying Yama rather than Indra as "lord of the gandharvas" — 1 further commentary, not merged into the count above since it takes a different position. `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-6-3`

Palden Sherab's own retinue-list assigns bhūta = retinue of the northeast "dbang ldan," vetāla = retinue of the southwest headed by Legden (cited above, Position C), and gandharva = retinue of Indra of the east — matching the majority dbang-ldan/rākṣasa/Indra pattern in its bhūta and gandharva assignments, while its vetāla assignment (Legden) is its own distinct position. `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-1-2` (already cited above under "Extended directional-guardian schemes" and Position C)

### Unique
Exemplar-naming without a full retinue-of-X framing: Drakpa Gyaltsen's word-by-word gloss names bhūta = Gaṇapati, gandharva = "the five-crested ones," in addition to the vetāla/yakṣa identifications already cited above; and glosses "the hosts" (ཚོགས) as "the eight classes of gods and spirits." `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-40`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-42`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-44`

Gendun Drub's matching exemplar-set (already cited above) additionally names bhūta = Gaṇapati and gandharva = "the five-crested ones," completing its three-way correspondence with Drakpa Gyaltsen (bhūta, vetāla, gandharva); its yakṣa exemplar (the son of Vaiśravaṇa) remains a distinct, related sub-position from Drakpa Gyaltsen's (Vaiśravaṇa himself) — see Divergences above. `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-2-1-1-5-3` (already cited above)

Gendun Gyatso's matching pair with Karma Maitri (already cited above) additionally names gandharva as headed by "Zur-bu-lnga-ba" ("Five-Crested"), the same figure-type as Drakpa Gyaltsen/Gendun Drub's exemplar under a different transliteration, and bhūta as headed by Gaṇapati. `gendun-gyatso:c-1-6-2` (already cited above)

Karma Maitri's fully atomized single-word glosses name Gaṇapati and generic "bhūta spirits" as separate items, and gandharva including "the five-crested ones." `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-8`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-9`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-6-11`

Taranatha's own glosses are unique in the corpus for their etymological and doctrinal range: bhūta = "the lord of hosts and others, obstructors who lead [beings] astray"; vetāla explained by etymology ("named for performing vetāla-activity... the deed's name applied to its doer"), identified with "the divine class of rākṣasas who have accomplished mantra-power... such as the nine Bhairavas"; gandharva = "Viśvāvasu and others" (a third, distinct exemplar-name not found elsewhere in this corpus); and — uniquely — an additional fifth class beyond the canonical four, "radiance-stealing devas and rākṣasīs." `taranatha:c-6-9`, `taranatha:c-6-10`, `taranatha:c-6-11`, `taranatha:c-6-13`

Tsultrim Namdak's gloss additionally names the gandharva-exemplar as "the gandharva-king Pañcaśikha" (ཟུར་ཕུད་ལྔ་པ) — the Sanskrit-derived proper name underlying the "five-crested" epithet used by Drakpa Gyaltsen, Gendun Drub, and Gendun Gyatso above, so this is recorded as the same figure under its fuller name rather than a new position. `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-22` (already cited above)

Konchok Thabkhe's own commentary gives a distinct etymological excursus on the charnel-ground guardians, "corpse-rider," and the pairing of gandharva with Yama (cited above), explicitly flagged by the author as tentative: "a point the author flags for further examination" (དཔྱད་བར་བྱའོ) — this epistemic hedge is preserved rather than upgraded to a settled claim, per Rule 14. `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-6-6`

Tenga Tulku gives a distinctive moral/psychological characterization of the four classes not found elsewhere in the corpus — bhūta as "preta-class beings who wander the sky, forever tormented by fear and suffering"; vetāla as corpses "reanimated as living [beings] by dark māras"; gandharva as beings "wandering the intermediate state... who always harbor harmful intent toward others"; and yakṣa as hosts "endowed with magical power and strength" — concluding that all of them attend her "with the thought of going for refuge." `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-2-1-1-5-4`

Pema Namgyal names spirit-exemplars within its benefit-statement (cited in full under "Practice and benefits," below): Gaṇapati (bhūta), "Kālabhairava" (a cannibal-demon exemplar for the vetāla-type class), "the horse-headed one" (a gandharva-king exemplar), and Vaiśravaṇa's retinue (yakṣa). `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-25`

Sungrab Tulku's concluding retinue-list statement (cited above) is echoed by Tsultrim Namdak's own closing line, that all these classes "heed your command and, with reverence, offer their praise" — a formulaic close not adding further identification. `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-23`

Tenzin Dhonzang's restatement (cited above under "The four principal worshipping gods") also names the four retinue-classes generically as worshipping "from before, reverently," without exemplars. `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-4` (already cited above)

## Iconographic form

### Consensus
None shared by all contributing commentaries — see Divergences. Only four of the sixteen commentaries in this packet give iconographic detail for this homage, falling into two distinct traditions.

### ⚑ Divergences
Two distinct iconographic traditions are attested:

**Position A — elaborate form (one face, four arms), red body:** seated in bodhisattva posture on a seat of red lotus and sun disc; one face, four arms; right hands hold a vajra and a sword, left hands form the threatening gesture (mudrā) and hold a lasso. Confirmed by this commentary's own citation of a further verse attributed to "Nyima Sbaspa": "Abiding on lotus and sun, red; one face, four arms, vajra and sword; holding the threatening gesture and lasso, accomplishing what is fitting" — near-identical to the commentary's own description, re-read and confirmed as a genuine corroborating citation rather than a loose paraphrase.

— attested: `yama-sonam:c-3-6-7`, `yama-sonam:c-3-6-8`, `yama-sonam:c-3-6-9` (1 commentary, 3 claims)

**Position B — "Great Terrifying One" form (one face, two hands), dark-red body, wrathful implement:** dark-red body; seated on a lotus-and-moon seat; one face, two hands; right hand in supreme-giving mudrā; left hand holds an utpala at whose centre is a vajra-dagger (phurba) blazing with a HŪṂ-marked fire-lasso and sparks. Sangye Nyentrul and Palden Sherab's descriptions correspond closely on this core imagery (re-read and confirmed as genuine, not merely topical: both specify dark-red colour, lotus-and-moon seat, right-hand supreme-giving mudrā, and the utpala-vajra-dagger-HŪṂ-fire combination), though Palden Sherab alone specifies the left hand's mudrā as additionally symbolizing the Three Jewels, and neither commentary's phrasing is verbatim-identical. Tsultrim Namdak's separate claim (in a distinct section of that commentary, keyed to the epithet "Great Terrifier / Destroyer of the Spirit-Classes" already cited above) independently attests the same tradition's core elements — dark-red body, holding a phurba, radiating HŪṂ-sparks — without detailing the mudrās or seat.

— attested (core: dark-red, lotus-moon, one face two hands, utpala + vajra-dagger + HŪṂ-fire): `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-1-1`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-1-2`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-2-1` (2 commentaries, 3 claims)
— attested (core elements only — dark-red, phurba, HŪṂ-sparks — without mudrā/seat detail): `tsultrim-namdak:c-3-9` (1 further commentary; 3 in total for Position B)

No commentary in this corpus reconciles these two traditions or states that one supersedes the other; they correspond to the two distinct naming traditions recorded under "Naming and epithets" above (the plain "Victorious over the Three Worlds" / "Utterly Victorious" epithets vs. the "Great Terrifier" epithet).

### Unique
None beyond the two positions above.

## Esoteric / hidden-meaning interpretation

### Consensus
Two commentaries independently give closely corresponding "hidden-meaning" (completion-stage-with-marks) readings, mapping the five principal worshippers onto the five elements and the four retinue-classes onto channel/drop/wind/thought, with dissolution of the winds into the indestructible drop identified as "worship" and "praise":

"བརྒྱ་བྱིན་ནི་ས་དང་། མེ་ལྷ་མེ་དང་། ཚངས་པ་ཆུ་དང་། རླུང་ལྷ་ནི་རླུང་དང་། སྣ་ཚོགས་དབང་ཕྱུག་ནི་ནམ་མཁའ་ཡིན་པས་ན།" (Palden Sherab) against "བརྒྱ་བྱིན་སོགས་ལྔ་ནི་འབྱུང་བ་ལྔ་སྟེ། བརྒྱ་བྱིན་ནི་ས། མེ་ལྷ་ནི་མེ། ཚངས་པ་ནི་ཆུ། རླུང་ལྷ་ནི་རླུང་། སྣ་ཚོགས་དབང་ཕྱུག་ནམ་མཁའ་འོ།" (Taranatha) — Indra=earth, Agni=fire, Brahmā=water, Vāyu=wind, Sarveśvara=space, in both. And for the retinue-classes: "འབྱུང་པོ་རྩ་དང་། རོ་ལངས་ཐིག་ལེ་དང་། དྲི་ཟ་རླུང་དང་། གནོད་སྦྱིན་རྣམ་རྟོག་སྟེ།" (Palden Sherab) against "འབྱུང་པོ་རྩ། རོ་ལངས་ཐིག་ལེ། དྲི་ཟ་རླུང་། གནོད་སྦྱིན་རྣམ་རྟོག་སྟེ།" (Taranatha) — bhūta=channel, vetāla=drop, gandharva=wind, yakṣa=conceptual thought, in both — re-read and confirmed as a genuine, near-verbatim correspondence rather than coincidental topical overlap, indicating a shared exegetical source (the same relationship already observed between these two commentaries at verse 4 and, within this verse, in the word-glosses for Agni and Brahmā cited above).

— attested: `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-3-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-3-2`, `taranatha:c-6-15`, `taranatha:c-6-16`, `taranatha:c-6-17`, `taranatha:c-6-18` (2 commentaries, 6 claims)

### ⚑ Divergences
None — the two commentaries' hidden-meaning readings are consistent with each other throughout this shared portion.

### Unique
Taranatha alone adds a concluding remark identifying this section of exegesis as teaching "the branch of dissolution" (a technical term from its own six-branch or similar completion-stage framework), not paralleled by Palden Sherab's version. `taranatha:c-6-19`

Palden Sherab alone continues into two further "hidden-meaning" tiers beyond the shared elemental/channel correspondence above: a completion-stage-*without*-marks reading identifying the five elements, five aggregates, twelve sense-fields, and eighteen constituents as, from the beginning, the maṇḍala itself — "great purity in the relative and great equality in the ultimate" — and a further elaboration identifying the elements as the five great mothers, the aggregates as the five buddha-families, and the sense-fields/constituents as heroes and heroines, purifying "all self-grasping and ninefold pride." No other commentary in this corpus gives either of these further tiers for this verse. `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-4-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-4-2`

## Practice and benefits

### Consensus
Two commentaries independently describe a closely corresponding generation-stage practice: visualizing that spirit-obstructors causing madness, forgetfulness/confusion, and paralysis are destroyed and prevented from rising again. Sangye Nyentrul: "the obstructing spirits causing madness (སྨྱོ་བྱེད), forgetfulness (རྗེད་བྱེད), paralysis (རེངས་བྱེད), and the like are destroyed and prevented from rising again." Palden Sherab (within its own generation-stage visualization, cited in full above under "Iconographic form"): the vajra-dagger's fire-noose "split[s] the heads of the spirit-obstructors of confusion (རྨོངས་བྱེད), madness-demons (སྨྱོ་འདྲེ), forgetfulness (བརྗེད་ངས), and so forth... unable to rise again." Re-read and confirmed: the two share the same three-part affliction-set (confusion/madness, a madness-demon, forgetfulness) and the same "unable to rise again" outcome, though the wording is not verbatim-identical.

— attested: `sangye-nyentrul:c-7-1-3`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-2-1` (2 commentaries; `palden-sherab:c-3-1-6-2-1` already cited above under "Iconographic form")

### ⚑ Divergences
None — the further benefit-statements below do not contradict the shared "subduing spirit-caused affliction" theme above; they are recorded as separate, non-overlapping elaborations.

### Unique
Tsultrim Namdak's separate claim (already cited above under "Naming and epithets" and "Iconographic form") independently states that she "overpowers and destroys the harm of obstructing spirits, misleading forces, the retinue of rākṣasas, zombies, piśācas, yakṣas, and lunatic-demon kings" — a related but broader practice-statement than Sangye Nyentrul's/Palden Sherab's madness/forgetfulness/paralysis framing, not merged with it since its own target-list is differently enumerated. `tsultrim-namdak:c-3-9` (already cited above)

Pema Namgyal states a distinct benefit: prostrating to her frees one "from the fear of those spirits in this life and in all lives" — naming as exemplars Gaṇapati (bhūta), "Kālabhairava" (a cannibal-demon), "the horse-headed one" (a gandharva-king), and Vaiśravaṇa's retinue (yakṣa). `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-25`

Tenzin Dhonzang gives an a fortiori argument (if even the great mundane gods prostrate, circumambulate, make offerings, and request attainments from her, "what need is there to mention us humans?"), and states that her chief enlightened activity is "pacifying harm from elemental spirits." `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-5`

Tenzin Dhonzang separately states that her enlightened activity is entrusted specifically to the "white-side" directional guardians, such that "those of the black side find no opening" when one entrusts activity to her. `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-8`

---

## Unique comparanda and asides

Tenzin Dhonzang alone brings in material from outside this verse: an etymology of "homage" tied to a doctrinal narrative in which Indra offers the Buddha a white conch and Brahmā offers a golden thousand-spoked wheel, urging him to turn the dharma-wheel of the four joys — identifying Indra and Brahmā as the traditional "requesters" of the Buddha's teaching, a narrative not found in any other commentary consulted for this topic. `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-6-3`
