## Structural placement and root-verse citation

### Consensus
All sixteen commentaries in the packet route content to this slot, but not with the same marker. Eight give an explicit global ordinal, "the twenty-first" (ཉེར་གཅིག་པ) or its numeral: `yama-sonam:c-3-21-1`, `gendun-gyatso:c-1-21-1`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-1`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-21-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-0-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-24-1` (marked "༼༢༡༽"), `taranatha:c-21-1-2`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-1`. Four instead give a *local* ordinal, "the sixth" (དྲུག་པ), because their own sa-bcad nests this verse as the sixth member of a "praise by way of the activity of destroying spirits and zombies" (གདོན་དང་རོ་ལངས་འཇོམས་པའི་འཕྲིན་ལས་ཀྱི་སྒོ་ནས་བསྟོད་པ) sub-group rather than numbering the twenty-one homages flatly: `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-3-6-1`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-6-1`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-3-6-1`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-6-1`. The remaining four (`drakpa-gyaltsen`, `pema-namgyal`, `sangye-nyentrul`, `tsultrim-namdak`) give no explicit ordinal marker within the claims in this packet — their content was routed to this slot by claim-content match (flagged ⓘ in the packet for `drakpa-gyaltsen`, `pema-namgyal`, and `tenga-tulku`), or by node title alone (`sangye-nyentrul`'s node is numbered "22" but its own content quotes verse "༢༡").

Ten commentaries quote the root verse's full four lines (whole or split across adjacent claims):

— full verse quoted attested: `yama-sonam:c-3-21-3`, `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-3-6-2`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-97`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-98`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-99`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-100`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-6-2`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-3-6-2`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-3-6-3`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-0-2`, `sungrab-tulku:c-24-2`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-6-2`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-2`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-71` (10 commentaries)

`gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-6-2`'s own claim title labels its quotation "Verse 22" even though the spine map routes it here as verse 21's content — an internal-numbering quirk of that commentary's own extraction, not evidence gendun-drub disagrees about which verse this is. `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-6-2` marks internal omissions with "…" rather than quoting the verse as one unbroken block. `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-71` quotes the four lines together with the two following lines that announce the "twenty-one" count (see "The concluding count of twenty-one," below).

The remaining six commentaries give only a partial or paraphrastic opening in this packet: `gendun-gyatso:c-1-21-1` (fragmentary — no verbatim སྒྲོལ་མ in the OCR'd fragment), `karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-1` (only the word "homage"), `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-21-2` (paraphrase, not a verbatim block), `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-0-1` (paraphrase), `taranatha:c-21-1-2` (abbreviates with "སོགས", "etc."), and `pema-namgyal` (does not quote the verse at all in this packet, being routed here entirely by claim-content match).

`tsultrim-namdak:c-3-24` gives a further paraphrase, opening with the verse's first line and continuing directly into iconographic and benefit content (see below) rather than a clean four-line quotation.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed — the ordinal-marker differences above reflect different levels of each commentary's own sa-bcad, not disagreement about which verse this is.

### Unique
`tsultrim-namdak:c-8-4` is a distinct case, flagged ⚑ ambiguous in the packet itself: it quotes the *entire* twenty-one-homage root praise as one continuous recitation block with no per-verse claim boundaries of its own (opening "OṂ, Venerable Lady, homage to Noble Tārā" and running through all twenty-one homages to the closing "twenty-one" line). Because this commentary does not gloss the praise verse by verse, the claim cannot be cleanly split among the twenty-one slots without dividing a single claim; it is carried through here, per the packet's own flag, as a single witness that this commentary transmits the full praise text including verse 21's four lines, without separately attesting anything specific to homage 21 beyond that transmission.

`karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-5` adds a structural note not paralleled elsewhere in this facet: "This praise is adorned with the root mantra, the peaceful-wrathful mantra taught earlier" — anticipating the "concluding count" material discussed below.

## Name, epithet, and classification of this homage

### Consensus
None — every commentary that supplies a proper name for this homage's Tārā gives a **different** one (or shares one with at most one other commentary); this facet, as in the parallel homage 9, is a set of divergent naming traditions rather than a shared consensus.

### ⚑ Divergences
At least four distinct naming/classification traditions are attested:

1. **"Tārā Who Fully Perfects" / "Who Brings to Full Completion"** (ཡོངས་སུ་རྫོགས་བྱེད་སྒྲོལ་མ) — `yama-sonam:c-3-21-1` (heading: རྗེ་བཙུན་མ་ཡོངས་སུ་རྫོགས་བྱེད་སྒྲོལ་མ), `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-6-3` ("she is called here 'Tārā Who Brings to Full Completion,'" འདི་ལ་ཡོངས་སུ་རྫོགས་བྱེད་སྒྲོལ་མ་ཞེས་འབྱུང་ངོ་། — the same core epithet ཡོངས་སུ་རྫོགས་བྱེད་སྒྲོལ་མ). Two commentaries.
2. **"[Jetsün Ārya] Radiant Goddess Who Restores/Redeems the Life-Force of the Sick"** (ནད་པའི་བླ་ཚེ་བར/བླུ་བྱེད་... འོད་ཟེར་ཅན་མ) — `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-0-1` ("Jetsün Ārya Tārā, Radiant Goddess, Who Restores [བར་བྱེད] the Life-Force of the Sick"), `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-1-1` ("Noble Radiant Goddess Who Redeems [བླུ་བྱེད] the Sick Person's Stolen Life-force" — a real lexical variant, བར་བྱེད vs བླུ་བྱེད, not the identical word), `tsultrim-namdak:c-3-24` (names her plainly "Sgrol-ma 'od-zer-can-ma," "Radiant One," without the "restores the sick's life-force" qualifier built into the name itself, though its own content describes exactly that function — see "Benefit," below). Three commentaries share the epithet family; only two share the qualifying clause verbatim in sense though not in wording.
3. **"Accomplisher of Enlightened Activity"** (འཕྲིན་ལས་སྒྲུབ་མཛད་མ) — `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-1`. Unique to this commentary.
4. **Functional/thematic classification** rather than a proper name — `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-21-1` labels the verse "homage by way of complete [buddha-]activity" (འཕྲིན་ལས་ཡོངས་རྫོགས་ཀྱི་སྒོ་ནས་བསྟོད་པ); `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-3-6-1`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-6-1`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-6-1`, and `sungrab-tulku:c-24-1` instead classify it by the specific activity of "destroying spirits and zombies" (already cited above under Structural placement).

`karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-6` reports a distinct identification not paralleled by any other claim in the packet: "the homage's object is Tārā who possesses twenty-one emanations" (རྣམ་འཕྲུལ་ཉི་ཤུ་རྩ་གཅིག་དང་ལྡན་པའི་སྒྲོལ་མ) — framing the object of homage 21 specifically as the deity possessing all twenty-one emanated forms, rather than naming a single distinct form for this verse. No other commentary in the packet echoes this framing.

The remaining commentaries (`drakpa-gyaltsen`, `gendun-gyatso`, `lobsang-dawa`, `pema-namgyal`, `taranatha`) supply no proper name or classificatory label for this homage's Tārā within the claims in this packet; `taranatha` instead structures its entire treatment via a four-level hermeneutic (see "Multi-level exegesis," below) rather than naming the deity.

### Unique
`karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-6` (twenty-one emanations, above) is unique to its own commentary.

## The three seed-syllables OṂ ĀḤ HŪṂ at crown, throat, and heart

### Consensus
Fifteen of the sixteen commentaries in the packet (all but `pema-namgyal`, whose claims in this packet concern the ten-syllable mantra rather than the three seed-syllables) describe the same basic content: the three "suchnesses" (དེ་ཉིད་གསུམ) of body, speech, and mind — the syllables OṂ, ĀḤ (or Ā), and HŪṂ — are placed at the crown, throat, and heart respectively, and by this arrangement she is genuinely endowed with the power to pacify harm/affliction/obstacles.

— attested: `yama-sonam:c-3-21-4`, `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-3-6-3`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-97`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-98`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-6-2`, `gendun-gyatso:c-1-21-1`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-2`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-21-2`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-3-6-2`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-1-1`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-0-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-24-3`, `taranatha:c-21-2-2`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-6-3`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-3`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-72` (15 commentaries)

Of these, four specify the syllables' colours — white OṂ, red ĀḤ, blue HŪṂ — a sub-detail not given by the rest: `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-1-1`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-0-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-24-3`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-6-3` (4 commentaries; not folded into the main consensus count above since the majority of the fifteen do not specify colour).

`palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-1-2` and `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-73` further specify that the resulting light pacifies obstacles at three levels — outer (formed/formless), inner (illness from disturbed elements), and secret (afflictive self-grasping/conceptual thought) — a matching three-way elaboration shared by these two claims specifically, not generalized to the wider consensus above.

### ⚑ Divergences
Whose crown, throat, and heart are these? `yama-sonam:c-3-21-5` states this as a live disagreement in the corpus: "Although some commentaries apply the placement of the three syllables to the practitioner's own three places, since this is the context of praising the goddess, it must instead be applied to the goddess's own three places, and this is also the tantra's own intent" (འགྲེལ་པ་འགའ་ཞིག་ལས་ཡི་གེ་གསུམ་སྒྲུབ་པ་པའི་གནས་གསུམ་ལ་བཀོད་པའི་དོན་དུ་སྦྱར་སྣང་ཡང་འདི་ལྷ་མོ་ལ་བསྟོད་པའི་སྐབས་ཡིན་པས་ལྷ་མོའི་གནས་གསུམ་ལ་བྱ་དགོས་ཤིང་). It supports this reading with a citation from Nyima Sbaspa (`yama-sonam:c-3-21-6`, continuing the argument that placing the three suchnesses at the goddess's own three places is what makes her "of great power").

Four commentaries in the packet explicitly instruct the *practitioner* to visualize the syllables at his or her own crown/throat/heart, as part of a self-generation sādhana: `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-1-1` ("The practitioner's own body, speech, and mind... are to be meditated as the essence of Jetsün Tārā's body, speech, and mind: at the crown... white OṂ..."), `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-0-1` ("at the practitioner's own crown, white OM..."), `sungrab-tulku:c-24-3` ("If the practitioner meditates by placing, at his own crown, the white syllable OṂ..."), `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-3` ("The yogi meditates, arraying at his own crown the suchness of body OṂ..."). Four commentaries, matching exactly the practice `yama-sonam` singles out as mistaken.

`drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-97` and `taranatha:c-21-2-2` instead describe the syllables as arrayed at the goddess's own body/generated form ("the three [seed-syllables] are arranged at her body, speech, and mind"; "in the meditative concentration that generates the form of Tārā, placing the suchness of the three seed-syllables in the three places"), aligning with `yama-sonam`'s stated position rather than the practitioner-self camp. The remaining commentaries in the Consensus list above do not specify explicitly enough, within the claims in this packet, to be assigned to either side, and are left unclassified on this specific point rather than forced to one camp.

### Unique
None beyond the positions above.

## Full-body iconography

### Consensus
None — this facet is a direct divergence between two iconographic traditions.

### ⚑ Divergences
**White, one face, three eyes, two arms, riding a bull, tiger-skin skirt, rosary and trident** — `yama-sonam:c-3-21-7` ("Her body colour is white; she has one face, three eyes, and two arms; she abides with legs half-crossed atop a bull, upon a maṇḍala of lotus and moon; she wears a tiger-skin skirt; her right hand holds a rosary, her left a trident"), supported by a citation from Nyima Sbaspa's own sādhana at `yama-sonam:c-3-21-8` ("Upon a bull born of HA, upon lotus and moon; seated legs half-crossed, wearing a tiger-skin skirt; one face, [eyes of] liberation, three eyes, hands with a pearl rosary and a three-pronged spear"). One commentary (internally consistent across two of its own claims plus a supporting citation).

**White, one face, two hands, right hand in supreme-giving mudrā, left holding an utpala topped with a golden fish** — `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-2-1` ("white-coloured, both legs in archer's posture, one face, two hands — right in supreme-giving mudrā, left, symbolizing the Three Jewels, holding an utpala's stem... atop its pollen-centre holding a golden fish"), `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-1-1` ("white body") + `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-1-2` ("one face, two hands — right in supreme-giving mudra, left holding an utpala atop which a golden fish, from which light rays radiate"), `tsultrim-namdak:c-3-24` ("white, holding an auspicious golden fish upon an utpala in the mudrā of refuge-giving"). Three commentaries.

No other commentary in the packet gives an explicit full-body iconography (colour, face/arm count, implements) for this homage; the rest describe only the three seed-syllables (see above) without addressing overall form.

### Unique
None beyond the two positions above.

## Classes of harmful spirits, zombies, and yakṣas destroyed

### Consensus
Five commentaries specifically enumerate "eighteen" (བཅོ་བརྒྱད) classes of graha-spirits/döns as the harm this homage destroys:

— attested: `dharmabhadra:c-1-2-3-6-4`, `lobsang-dawa:c-1-2-3-6-3`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-1-3`, `pema-namgyal:c-2-4-55`, `taranatha:c-21-1-4` (5 commentaries)

A broader group states the general destructive/protective activity (against spirits, zombie-sorcery, and yakṣa-harm) without specifying "eighteen" or any other count: `yama-sonam:c-3-21-4`, `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-99`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-6-2`, `gendun-gyatso:c-1-21-2`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-3`, `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-21-3`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-0-2`, `sungrab-tulku:c-24-4`, `taranatha:c-21-1-5`, `taranatha:c-21-1-6`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-6-4`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-7`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-74` (13 commentaries; not folded into the "eighteen" count above since they do not state that specific number).

### ⚑ Divergences
`tenzin-dhonzang` alone gives two further, different enumerations, neither matching the "eighteen classes" tradition above: **fifteen great spirits afflicting children** (`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-4`, naming the Gentle One, Deer-King, Thirst-Causer, and others; continued at `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-5` with the Guardian, Displeasure-Causer, and others), and, quoting the *White Parasol* (Sitātapatrā) sūtra, **twenty-eight harmful spirits** spanning gods, nāgas, asuras, yakṣas, rākṣasas, and other classes (`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-6`). No other commentary in the packet corroborates either the fifteen-fold or the twenty-eight-fold count; `tenzin-dhonzang` treats these as its own supplementary enumerations rather than glosses of "eighteen classes."

### Unique
`dharmabhadra:c-1-2-3-6-7` reports a variant fourth-line rendering of the root verse attributed to "the venerable protector of beings" (རྗེ་བརྩུན་འགྲོ་བའི་མགོན་པོ), reading "she who is the very supreme and excellent destroyer, swiftly" (མྱུར་དུ་འཇོམས་པའི་མཆོག་རབ་ཉིད་མ) in place of the root text's own fourth line — a textual-variant note not paralleled by any other claim in this packet.

`konchok-thabkhe:c-2-21-3` additionally frames this verse as "the concluding summary of all the supreme and ordinary liturgical sections taught above" (མཆོག་ཐུན་གྱི་ལས་ཚོགས་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་མཇུག་བསྡུས་པའོ) — a structural observation about the verse's place in the text as a whole, not paralleled elsewhere in this facet.

## The ten-syllable mantra (Oṃ tāre tuttāre ture svāhā)

### Consensus
Four commentaries explicitly name the destroying mantra as the "ten-syllable" mantra:

— attested: `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-100`, `gendun-gyatso:c-1-21-2`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-4`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-7` (4 commentaries)

`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-7` gives the mantra in full: ཨོཾ་ཏཱ་རེ་ཏུ་ཏྟཱ་རེ་ཏུ་རེ་སྭ་ཧཱ (Oṃ tāre tuttāre ture svāhā).

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed as disagreement.

### Unique
`pema-namgyal:c-2-4-54` gives a distinct, non-"ten-syllable" framing of the same underlying mantra: threefold repetition of TĀRE TUTTĀRE TURE with OṂ at the head and SVĀHĀ at the end, described as "seven syllables" tripled ("དེ་ཉིད་སུམ་སྤེལ") rather than as a fixed ten-syllable formula, "arranged threefold specifically to accomplish pacifying activities against illness, spirits, and misdeeds/obscurations." No other claim in the packet frames the mantra this way.

`taranatha:c-21-1-7` states that the destruction of harm is accomplished "by the power of the mantra 'rab mchog ture'" without naming it as ten-syllable specifically, and `taranatha:c-21-1-8` proposes an alternate etymological reading of "Ture" as "Swift One" with a suggested textual emendation (see "Practice and etymology," below).

— attested: `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-1-3`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-2-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-2-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-1-1`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-22-1-3`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-3-24` (3 commentaries)

None observed — no other commentary in the packet addresses this specific benefit, whether to affirm or contest it.

### Unique

## Multi-level exegesis of the three suchnesses

### Consensus
Two commentaries structure their entire treatment of this verse through an explicit four-level hermeneutic, and their labels for the levels closely parallel each other: `taranatha` announces "the literal meaning, the general meaning, the hidden meaning, and the ultimate meaning" (ཡི་གེའི་དོན་དང་། སྤྱིའི་དོན་དང་། སྦས་དོན་དང་། མཐར་ཐུག་གི་དོན, `taranatha:c-21-1-2`), while `palden-sherab` labels its own four sections "the word-meaning" (`palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-1-1`), "the general meaning, according to the generation-stage" (སྤྱི་དོན་བསྐྱེད་རིམ་ལྟར, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-2-1`), "the hidden meaning, according to the completion-stage-with-marks" (སྦས་དོན་རྫོགས་རིམ་མཚན་བཅས, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-3-1`), and "the ultimate meaning, according to the completion-stage-without-marks" (མཐར་ཐུག་མཚན་མེད་རྫོགས་རིམ, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-4-1`) — the same four-part structure (word/general/hidden/ultimate), independently applied.

— attested: `taranatha:c-21-1-1`, `taranatha:c-21-1-2`, `taranatha:c-21-2-1`, `taranatha:c-21-3-1`, `taranatha:c-21-4-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-1-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-2-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-3-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-4-1` (2 commentaries)

At the **hidden-meaning** level, the two commentaries' core equation is near character-identical: `taranatha:c-21-3-4` states "'Spirits' is the channels; 'vetāla' is the drops; 'yakṣa' is conceptual thoughts — these having been overcome into the expanse of clear light" (གདོན་ཏེ་རྩ་དང་། རོ་ལངས་ཏེ་ཐིག་ལེ་དང་། །གནོད་སྦྱིན་ཏེ་རྣམ་པར་རྟོག་པ་རྣམས་འོད་གསལ་གྱི་དབྱིངས་སུ་བཅོམ་པ་ལས), and `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-3-2` states "'Spirits' are channels, 'zombies' are drops, 'yakṣas' are conceptual thought; destroying these into the expanse of clear light" (གདོན་ནི་རྩ་དང་། རོ་ལངས་ནི་ཐིག་ལེ་དང་། གནོད་སྦྱིན་ནི་རྣམ་པར་རྟོག་པ་སྟེ་དེ་རྣམས་འོད་གསལ་དབྱིངས་སུ་བཅོམ་པས) — differing only in the particle ཏེ vs ནི, otherwise matching wording throughout: རྩ་ (channels) / ཐིག་ལེ་ (drops) / རྣམ་པར་རྟོག་པ་ (conceptual thought) / འོད་གསལ(་གྱི་)དབྱིངས་སུ་བཅོམ་པ (destroyed into the expanse of clear light).

— hidden-meaning channels/drops/thoughts equation attested: `taranatha:c-21-3-4`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-3-2` (2 commentaries)

### ⚑ Divergences
The two commentaries' **hidden-meaning** framing around this shared equation differs: `taranatha:c-21-3-2`–`taranatha:c-21-3-3` frames the three suchnesses as the three visions of appearance, increase, and near-attainment (སྣང་བ་དང་མཆེད་པ་དང་། ཉེ་བར་ཐོབ་པ), arising in sequence toward momentary peace/emptiness approaching clear light, and `taranatha:c-21-3-5` glosses "supreme" as "swiftly arising in the divine form"; `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-3-1` instead frames the same triad as the three visions of the *bardo of dharmatā* specifically — the white drop's moonrise-like appearance, the red drop's flame-like appearance, and their mingling into pitch-darkness — and `palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-3-2` glosses "peace" as clear-emptiness dharmadhātu and "supreme TURE" as the sole co-emergent bliss-emptiness wisdom. The underlying triad-equation is shared (above); the surrounding doctrinal framing (rising-toward-clear-light generation-stage completion vs. the specific three-bardo-visions schema) is not identical between the two.

At the **ultimate-meaning** level the two commentaries diverge further, into two different doctrinal frameworks rather than a shared reading:

- `taranatha` frames the ultimate meaning through **vajra-yoga** (`taranatha:c-21-4-2`–`taranatha:c-21-4-9`): the three vajras of body, speech, and mind are primordially the nature of all phenomena; she is endowed with the power to attain peace through the path since primordially pure; resting evenly in the expanse of the inseparable three vajras, one swiftly overcomes spirit-, vetāla-, and yakṣa-like sufferings; "supreme" is the sole innate wisdom of great bliss; the literal explanation, thus understood, praises Bhagavatī's emanation-body as renowned to dwell at the divine kings' abodes and Mount Potala, and, in actual meaning, is also a praise of the saṃbhogakāya and dharmakāya. `taranatha` further supplies an extended methodological note on this reading's sourcing: these definitive meanings follow the profound six-branch vajra-yoga (`taranatha:c-21-4-10`); they follow two Indian commentaries on the definitive names of the Tārodbhava-tantra (`taranatha:c-21-4-11`), mostly according with Dīpaṃkaraśrī's great tantra-commentary (`taranatha:c-21-4-12`); where instructions differ slightly, resolution proceeds from the tantra's own ultimate meaning (`taranatha:c-21-4-13`), per the principle that "a tantra must be understood through another tantra" (`taranatha:c-21-4-14`); the author states this is his own explanation, in accordance with vajra-yoga (`taranatha:c-21-4-15`); further useful correspondences exist between specific verse-phrases (of the wider praise, not only this verse) and elements of the generation-stage ritual, the five manifest-awakenings, and the deity-empowerment (`taranatha:c-21-4-16`), and between other phrases and the four empowerments, fire-offerings for the four activities and siddhis, and consecration (`taranatha:c-21-4-17`, `taranatha:c-21-4-18`, `taranatha:c-21-4-19`); the text also teaches the "peaceful by day, wrathful by night" sādhana (`taranatha:c-21-4-20`) and the famous twenty-one sādhanas and twenty-one activity-collections (`taranatha:c-21-4-21`); the author declines to elaborate further, as it would become too extensive (`taranatha:c-21-4-22`).
- `palden-sherab` frames the ultimate meaning instead through a **Great-Perfection-styled** completion-stage-without-marks (`palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-4-1`–`palden-sherab:c-3-1-21-4-2`): the three suchnesses are the three doors of liberation (signlessness, wishlessness, emptiness), self-aware primordial wisdom whose essence is, from the start, the three kāyas (empty dharmakāya, luminous saṃbhogakāya, unceasing nirmāṇakāya); recognizing and relaxing in this unfabricated state establishes fortunate disciples in the primordial state within a single lifetime; "peaceful, endowed with power" is the self-liberation of dualistic obscuration like ice melting into water; spirits/zombies/yakṣas are here glossed as suffering/karma/affliction respectively (a different triad-mapping from the hidden-meaning channels/drops/thoughts equation above); and "TURE, supreme" is buddhahood through instant recognition-liberation without meditation, for the sharpest-faculty disciples.

These are two independent, non-overlapping doctrinal elaborations of "ultimate meaning" — one via tantric vajra-yoga completion-stage-with-marks reasoning and citation of Indian tantra-commentaries, the other via a Dzogchen-styled completion-stage-without-marks framing — not a corroboration of one another, and not merged here.

### Unique
`taranatha:c-21-1-3` gives a distinct **literal-level** reading not paralleled by any other commentary's literal/word-meaning gloss in this packet: rather than identifying "the three suchnesses" at the literal level with the three seed-syllables (as every other commentary's opening gloss does — see "The three seed-syllables," above), taranatha's literal level identifies them with emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness directly, by which the disciples' minds are established and all obscurations abandoned. The seed-syllable/generation-stage reading appears in taranatha's structure only at the *second* ("general") level (`taranatha:c-21-2-2`, cited above), not the first.

`taranatha:c-21-1-8` proposes an alternate etymological reading of "Ture" as "Swift One," with a suggested textual emendation to "the very supreme one who swiftly destroys" — see "Practice and etymology," below.

`taranatha:c-21-2-3` adds, at the general-meaning level, that the passage also teaches a pith instruction for protecting against harm from spirits and so on — a brief practical note not elaborated further in the claim.

`taranatha:c-21-3-6`–`taranatha:c-21-3-9` supplies a proof-citation chain for the hidden-meaning reading: a citation is introduced from the Pradīpoddyotana (`taranatha:c-21-3-6`), quoted (`taranatha:c-21-3-7`, on clear light, union, and the two aspects of "the limit"), attributed to the intent of the Ārya Guhyasamāja-cycle tradition specifically as an ultimate-meaning explanation (`taranatha:c-21-3-8`), with the further note that a hidden meaning should also generally be understood to exist here (`taranatha:c-21-3-9`) — an attribution chain not paralleled by `palden-sherab` or any other commentary.

`konchok-thabkhe:c-2-21-4` offers a related but distinct doctrinal reading, quoting a mahāsiddha's verse that identifies the three suchnesses with deity, mantra, and samādhi (ལྷ་དང་སྔགས་དང་ཏིང་འཛིན), explained as meditating on the deity's body, reciting mantra, and cultivating the two-stage samādhi to accomplish the victor's three kāyas. This shares the general theme of "three suchnesses → three kāyas" with `taranatha`'s and `palden-sherab`'s completion-stage readings but uses a different vocabulary (deity/mantra/samādhi, not channels/drops/thoughts, and not the doors-of-liberation triad); it is not treated as corroborating either.

## The concluding count of twenty-one homages

### Consensus
None as a single mechanism — this facet is a genuine divergence over how the closing two lines' statement that "the praise together with the root mantra, and the homages, are twenty-one" is to be explained.

### ⚑ Divergences
1. **The peaceful and wrathful root mantras, combined, yield the twenty-one verses of praise.** `yama-sonam:c-3-21-9` quotes the closing lines and (`yama-sonam:c-3-21-10`) explains: "Just as the root mantra, comprising both peaceful and wrathful forms combined, yields twenty-one verses in this praise, so too the homages number twenty-one." `gendun-gyatso:c-1-21-3` states more briefly that these two lines "are the concluding words of the praise, [marking] the completion of the [twenty-one] homages," without stating the mechanism. `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-10` quotes the same closing lines and `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-11` supplies the specific arithmetic: the peaceful root mantra (Oṃ tāre tuttāre ture svāhā) plus the wrathful root mantra's fourteen syllables (Oṃ nama tāre namo hare hūṃ hare svāhā), to which five further syllables (hara hara phaṭ) are added, together yield "twenty-one verses of praise for the Twenty-One Tārās, and... twenty-one instances of 'homage.'" Three commentaries state the general peaceful-plus-wrathful link (`yama-sonam`, `gendun-gyatso`, `tenzin-dhonzang`); only `tenzin-dhonzang` supplies the specific syllable-count mechanism — not folded into a single attestation count per Rule 9, since `yama-sonam` and `gendun-gyatso` do not themselves state that arithmetic.
2. **The final verse is homage via the three root-mantra seed-syllables, distinct from the peaceful/wrathful mantra count.** `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-22-1` announces the topic ("the meaning of the final two lines"); `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-22-2` reports two rival views on what "and this" (འདི་དང) refers to — the root mantras taught earlier, or a separate four-line Tārā root mantra — and rejects the latter, since it would leave a set of twenty homages unaccounted for; `konchok-thabkhe:c-2-22-3` then gives the author's own view ("in my estimation"): the final verse is homage by way of the three seed-syllables (root of all mantras), citing the Pradīpoddyotana's teaching on root-syllables from the Guhyasamāja cycle, so that this final three-syllable homage, added to the twenty already given, makes twenty-one — "which accords very well with the tantra's own wording." This is a distinct mechanism from position 1: no shared syllable-arithmetic, and no other commentary in the packet corroborates it.

No claim in the packet reconciles positions 1 and 2, or acknowledges that the other exists.

### Unique
None beyond the two positions above.

## Practice and etymology

### Consensus
None — the claims below are independent notes from different commentaries, not corroborating each other.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-8` defines "mental recitation" (ཡིད་བཟླས) in detail as vajra-recitation of OṂ ĀḤ HŪṂ without moving the lips or tongue, the natural resonance of the three movements of wind (rising/entering/abiding) joined inseparably with the resonance of the three seed-syllables; `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-9` continues with the emanation and reabsorption of Tārā's maṇḍala through the resonance of HŪṂ/OṂ/ĀḤ respectively, noting the benefit that the mind does not become distracted and the mantra-garland and visualization arise clearly. No other commentary in the packet gives comparably detailed mental-recitation instructions keyed to this verse.

`sangye-nyentrul:c-22-0-3` glosses "Ture" (ཏཱུ་རེ) as "Swift One" (མྱུར་མ), she whose nature is supreme, deep loving-kindness. `taranatha:c-21-1-8` gives a related but distinct etymological note, proposing that "Ture" can also carry the sense "Swift One" and suggesting the verse would read more naturally emended to "the very supreme one who swiftly destroys" (མྱུར་དུ་འཇོམས་པའི་མཆོག་རབ་ཉིད་མ) — a textual-emendation proposal not found in `sangye-nyentrul`'s simple gloss. `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-74` gives a third variant: "Ture": she whose mind is extremely swift, and who, with supreme tender compassion, diligently works for beings' welfare during all six periods of day and night — closer in spirit to `sangye-nyentrul`'s reading (swiftness + supreme compassion) than to `taranatha`'s textual-emendation note, but independently worded and not claimed here as a verbatim match.

`pema-namgyal:c-2-4-56` quotes two verses "from the Buddha's own mouth" naming eight fears (lion, elephant, fire, snake, thief, iron-fetter, ocean, flesh-eating-demon) that Tārā pacifies, and applies this proof-citation to identify her here as "the one who most swiftly and supremely destroys all harm, Ture" — a citation not paralleled by any other claim in this packet at this slot.
