## Etymology and typology of mantra

### Consensus
None — this entire facet is attested by a single commentary.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-18-5`–`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-18-8` give the packet's only sustained generic doctrinal excursus on what "mantra" (*sngags*) itself means, in four continuous claims. In general, "mantra" is so called because it protects (*srung*) all beings' minds from suffering together with its causes, or protects the mind from ordinary appearance-clinging (`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-18-5`). The commentary then builds a cascading typology, each term named for a different quality of the same mantra: "essence" (*snying po*) is wisdom realizing emptiness conjoined with meditative equipoise; conjoined with wisdom and compassion equally it is "secret mantra" (*gsang sngags*); conjoined with wisdom and primordial awareness equally it is "awareness-mantra" (*rig sngags*) (`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-18-5`). Continuing what the claim attributes to "the great Jowo" (Atiśa), conjoined with wisdom and mindfulness equally it is truly "dhāraṇī-mantra" (*gzungs sngags*); and, restating the same four qualities from the side of function rather than conjunction, it is called "essence" because it arouses the deity's own essence (the ultimate wisdom of profundity and clarity inseparable), "secret mantra" because it possesses inconceivable power and function, "awareness-mantra" because it generates the awareness of primordial wisdom, and "dhāraṇī-mantra" because it generates unforgetting mindfulness of bodhicitta and so forth (`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-18-6`). The same passage continues into two further named categories not given in the fourfold list: because it arouses the deities' minds' wisdom of great bliss it is "essence-mantra" (*snying po*); because it further re-arouses that, "near-essence mantra" (*nye snying*); and because it becomes the root of all attainments, "root mantra" or "garland mantra" (*rtsa sngags* / *'phreng sngags*) — glossed in general as the vast stages of the path and, in particular, chiefly bodhicitta, with its own essence identified as the pure and impure illusory body (`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-18-7`). The passage's final layer nests the illusory body's own essence as the two form-bodies (*rūpakāya*); the profound stages of the path in general and, in particular, chiefly the correct view; the essence of that as the example and actual clear light; and the essence of that, in turn, as the wisdom-dharmakāya and svabhāvikakāya — concluding that it is excellent to recite the mantra having understood each of these functions (`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-18-8`).

No other commentary's claims in this packet offer a comparable generic definition or typology of mantra; per Q16, this typology is not corroborated elsewhere in the packet. It is worth noting, as a page-level observation only (not stated by any claim itself), that the term this same passage names "root mantra" or "garland mantra" (*rtsa sngags* / *'phreng sngags*) shares its first element with *rtsa ba'i sngags*, "the root mantra," the recurring name several other commentaries give to a specific portion of this text's own praise (see "'Root mantra' as the praise's own structural category," below) — the two usages are never cross-referenced by any claim in the packet, and this page does not treat them as the same claim.

## Origin: who spoke the mantra, and its mythic source

### Consensus
Two commentaries independently state that the Bhagavān (the Buddha) himself spoke the dhāraṇī-mantra as part of delivering the praise:

— attested: `gendun-drub:c-1-3`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-1-13` (2 commentaries)

`gendun-drub:c-1-3` states simply, "Then the Bhagavān spoke the dhāraṇī-mantra of praise." `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-1-13` gives the same event within its frame narrative — the Bhagavān instructing Mañjuśrī to hold the praise in mind, "then the Bhagavān spoke this [praise] together with its dhāraṇī-mantra."

### ⚑ Divergences
Two further claims name a different speaker or a different causal direction, without directly contradicting the Consensus claims above (which describe the frame narrative of the praise's delivery, not necessarily the deepest origin of the mantra itself):

- **The mantra as Ārya Tārā's own utterance** — `taranatha:c-7-3` names "the vidyā-mantra spoken by Ārya Tārā" — attributing the mantra's speech-act to the goddess herself, not to the Buddha of the frame narrative.
- **The mantra as arising from the goddess's own voice** — `tsultrim-namdak:c-1-17` gives a mythic-origin account, independent of its own commentary's frame-narrative claim above: "from the melody of her Brahmā-voice, like the kalapiṅka bird's song, arose the dhāraṇī-mantra of Tārā [tāra]," adding that mere recitation of it grants protection from all fears of this life and the next.

These are recorded as distinct data points rather than a two-sided dispute, since no claim states that its account excludes the others — a Buddha-delivers-the-teaching frame narrative and a goddess's-own-voice origin myth are not obviously incompatible within the same commentary (tsultrim-namdak's own two claims hold both), and neither this page nor any claim in the packet states how they relate.

### Unique
`taranatha:c-16-5` gives the reverse causal direction from either account above: the goddess herself "arose from the arrangement of the [ten-syllable] mantra" — the mantra as the source of the deity's manifestation, rather than the deity's voice as the source of the mantra. Read together, `taranatha:c-7-3` (mantra spoken by Tārā) and `taranatha:c-16-5` (Tārā arising from the mantra) could describe a non-dual relation between goddess and mantra of the kind found elsewhere in tantric doctrine — but this is this page's own observation; neither claim states such a relation itself, and no other commentary's claims in this packet address the question.

## "Root mantra" (རྩ་བའི་སྔགས།) as the praise's own structural category

### Consensus
Several commentaries gloss the root text's own recapitulating line — "this praise by the root mantra, and the twenty-one homages" (རྩ་བའི་སྔགས་ཀྱིས་བསྟོད་པ་འདི་དང་། ཕྱག་འཚལ་བ་ནི་ཉི་ཤུ་རྩ་གཅིག) — explicitly naming *both* "the root mantra" and "the twenty-one homages" as the praise's two structural halves:

— attested: `gendun-drub:c-2-2-0-6`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-22-2`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-22-3`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-23-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-25-2`, `tenga-tulku:c-1-2-3-6-6` (5 commentaries)

`gendun-drub:c-2-2-0-6` gives the clearest definitional gloss, attributed to "the gurus": "'praise by the root mantra' here means these twenty words of praise interwoven with the mantra taught here, and the 'twenty-one homages' are the twenty-one homages interwoven with that." `sangye-nyentrul:c-23-1` and `palden-sherab:c-3-1-22-3` both draw the further doctrinal conclusion that the root-mantra praise together with the twenty-one homages displays, complete within this text, the twenty-one emanations of Ārya Tārā.

`taranatha:c-22-1-10` similarly names "the root mantra" as the vehicle through which the praise is offered ("by way of these meanings, praise is offered — this is the praise by means of the root mantra"), but this claim's own quoted text does not pair the term with "the twenty-one homages," so it is noted here as a narrower, partial data point rather than folded into the five-commentary count above (per the full-statement-support rule).

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`palden-sherab:c-1-1` shows the same naming convention operating at the level of the text's own title, independent of the recapitulating verse: the Sanskrit and Tibetan titles quoted in this claim both name the text itself *Āryatāre-mantra-mūla-stotra-nāma-caikarika-viṃśatikā-nāma* / "The Praise and Homage to the Twenty-One [Verses] of the Root Mantra of Noble Tārā" — "root mantra" (Skt. *mantra-mūla*, Tib. *rtsa ba'i sngags*) appears already in the work's own title, not only in its closing recapitulation.

## What the "root mantra" identifies

### Consensus
Several commentaries identify the content of "the root mantra" with the peaceful and wrathful mantras taught earlier in the text:

— attested: `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-101`, `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-1-1`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-5`, `sungrab-tulku:c-25-3` (4 commentaries)

`drakpa-gyaltsen:c-1-101` glosses "this praise of the root mantra" as "this praise of [her] peaceful and wrathful forms." `karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-5` states the praise is "adorned with the root mantra, the peaceful-wrathful mantra taught earlier." `gendun-drub:c-2-2-2-3-1-1` places "the activity of the peaceful and wrathful mantras" as the first of a six-homage division of "praise by way of activity." `sungrab-tulku:c-25-3` gives the fullest identification, naming both formulas outright and calling the verse "praise by way of the power and activity of the two name-mantras" — the peaceful name-mantra and the wrathful name-mantra. This page does not reproduce or gloss the two formulas' own syllables here: that decoding belongs to the sibling `tara-mantra` topic page; only the identification-act itself (that "root mantra" names this peaceful/wrathful pairing) is in scope on this page.

### ⚑ Divergences
`gendun-drub:c-2-2-0-2` reports, attributed to unnamed "some" scholars and without clear endorsement, an entirely different identification of what "praise by the root mantra" names — a longer formula beginning namaḥ sarva tathāgatānāṃ. This stands as a reported minority reading against the four-commentary peaceful/wrathful identification above; as with the consensus claims, this page does not reproduce the alternate formula's own content in full, since decoding mantra text is `tara-mantra`'s scope — it is recorded here only as evidence that commentators disagreed over what the category "root mantra" refers to.

### Unique
None beyond the divergence above.

## Mantra's power against hostile mantras and sorcery

### Consensus
Several commentaries — largely glossing the same homage-verse, on Tārā as "Destroyer of the Power of Enemies' and Sorcerers' [Vidyā-]Mantras" — attribute to the mantra (or to mantra-and-truth-speech more broadly) the power to destroy or pacify enemies' and sorcerers' hostile mantras, sorcery-forms, curses, and magical devices:

— attested: `palden-sherab:c-3-1-16-0-1`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-16-1-1`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-17-0-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-17-1-1`, `sungrab-tulku:c-10-4`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-7` (4 commentaries)

`palden-sherab:c-3-1-16-0-1` and `sangye-nyentrul:c-17-1-1` both identify the verse's subject by essentially the same epithet — "Destroyer of the Power of Enemies' and Sorcerers' [Vidyā-]Mantras." `palden-sherab:c-3-1-16-1-1` elaborates that Ārya Tārā's light "utterly destroys the mantric power and verbal potency of sorcery"; `sangye-nyentrul:c-17-0-2` that light rays arising from HŪM at the heart "destroy the power of all the enemy's evil mantras and obstacles." `sungrab-tulku:c-10-4` names two wrathful sounds (TRAD, PHAT) by which Tārā "instantly destroys or pacifies all the power of the harmful-minded enemies' sorcery-forms, evil mantras, and evil-wrought magical devices." `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-21-7` extends the same theme to a different threat — the ten-syllable mantra swiftly destroying charnel-ground zombies sent to perform sorcery, and yakṣas who steal one's vital luster.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
None beyond the elaborations above.

## Mantra as protective armor (སྔགས་ཀྱི་གོ་ཆ)

### Consensus
Several commentaries describe the mantra — typically paired with mudrā — as functioning as protective "armor," generating joy and power and dispelling disputes, curses, quarrels, and bad dream-omens:

— attested: `karma-maitri:c-1-1-19-4`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-19-1-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-20-0-2`, `taranatha:c-19-6`, `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-19-4` (5 commentaries)

`karma-maitri:c-1-1-19-4` states simply that Tārā is "endowed with the armor of mantra." `taranatha:c-19-6` describes the practitioner as "fully bound by the armor of the mantra and mudrā you [the goddess] have blessed." `tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-19-4` has the practitioner "dons this armor" by reciting the mantra, with the result that "by the splendor of utmost joy, she dispels all quarrels from others and all bad dreams." `palden-sherab:c-3-1-19-1-2` and `sangye-nyentrul:c-20-0-2` both glose the same verse (on Bhagavatī Uṣṇīṣasitātapatrā, the White-Umbrella goddess, rather than Tārā's own generic form) in near-identical terms — mantra-and-mudrā armor generating joy that blazes through the practitioner's "three doors" (body, speech, mind) and dispelling opponents' disputes and bad dream-signs. Both claims still attest the same armor-metaphor for mantra, applied within this Tārā commentarial tradition to a closely associated deity, so they are counted in the consensus above.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`tenzin-dhonzang:c-4-19-4` frames the armor as donned through a specific triad of perceptions not paralleled by any other claim in this facet — the perception of deity as regards one's own body, the perception of mantra as regards speech, and the perception of dharmakāya as regards mind, held without separation while reciting the mantra.

## Benefit: reciting or recollecting the mantra purifies sin, poison, illness, and spirit-harm

### Consensus
Across several homage-verses, commentaries attribute purifying and protective power specifically to the mantra itself — reciting it, or merely recollecting it (alone, or together with the praise and deity) — destroying sin and its results, dispelling poison, and healing epidemic illness and spirit-harm:

— attested: `drakpa-gyaltsen:c-2-11`, `pema-namgyal:c-3-3`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-16-0-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-19-0-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-21-0-2`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-15-8`, `taranatha:c-22-1-30`, `taranatha:c-22-1-33`, `palden-sherab:c-3-1-20-1-2`, `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-25` (7 commentaries)

`drakpa-gyaltsen:c-2-11` states that "by recollecting the praise, the deity, and the mantra, poisons are cleared" — the mantra named as one of three objects of recollection, alongside the praise and the deity. `pema-namgyal:c-3-3` isolates the mantra alone: "merely by recollecting the awareness-mantra, the poison is thoroughly cleared and even transformed into nectar" (within a longer claim that also conditions this on prior recitation, treated fully under the `benefits` topic page). `taranatha:c-22-1-30` gives the same isolated claim: "merely by recollecting Tārā's vidyā-mantra," poison's "harm is removed, and it becomes like nectar." `karma-maitri:c-1-1-15-8` and `sangye-nyentrul:c-16-0-2` both attribute the destruction of sin/wrongdoing specifically to the mantra ("by means of this mantra she is able to destroy all great sins and obscurations"; the ten-syllable mantra, recited "according to the ritual with intense remorse," destroys "the great suffering that is the result of great wrongdoing"). `sangye-nyentrul:c-19-0-2`, `sangye-nyentrul:c-21-0-2`, and `palden-sherab:c-3-1-20-1-2` each attribute dispelling of poison or epidemic illness to reciting the mantra. `taranatha:c-22-1-33` and `tsultrim-namdak:c-2-1-2-1-25` both attribute pacification of spirit-harm and illness/epidemic to "the power of this vidyā-mantra" and to "the secret mantra" respectively.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
None beyond the elaborations above.

## Mantra's power: subjugation, summoning, and manifestation-shift

### Consensus
Three commentaries attribute to the mantra's own power (rather than to the deity's blessing in general) the ability to subjugate or summon beings under the goddess's control:

— attested: `gendun-gyatso:c-1-5-2`, `karma-maitri:c-1-1-5-7`, `taranatha:c-11-3` (3 commentaries)

`gendun-gyatso:c-1-5-2` and `karma-maitri:c-1-1-5-7` both gloss the same homage-verse in near-identical terms: Tārā "brings all [seven worlds] without exception under her power through the might of the vidyā-mantra" / "by the power of her vidyā-mantra draws all beings under her power without exception." `taranatha:c-11-3` extends the theme to a more specific class of beings: the goddess "by whose vidyā-mantra the host of kings who protect the earth, or the earth-goddesses" are summoned.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`karma-maitri:c-1-1-21-4` attributes a further, unspecified destructive power to the mantra — Tārā "accomplishes destruction by means of the ten-syllable mantra" — without this particular claim naming the object destroyed, so it is recorded separately from the subjugation consensus above rather than folded into it.

`taranatha:c-8-3` records a distinct and striking doctrinal point not paralleled elsewhere in the packet: "by the power of reciting the vidyā-mantra OM TURE and so on, from the naturally peaceful expanse Tārā herself comes to manifest in a form of great terror" — the mantra's recitation is here credited with effecting a shift in the goddess's own manifest form, from peaceful to wrathful, not merely with acting on external beings or obstacles.

## Mantra in iconography, body-placement, and practice

### Consensus
None — every claim in this facet is a single commentary's own treatment of mantra syllables or their placement; no two claims state an identical practice, though several instantiate the shared general Vajrayana convention of placing mantra syllables within body-visualization at the generation stage.

### ⚑ Divergences
None observed.

### Unique
`sangye-nyentrul:c-22-0-1` places three seed-syllables at three places on the practitioner's own body — white OM at the crown, red AH at the throat, blue HŪM at the heart — such that, because "the Noble Lady's deity-mantra-samādhi's three suchnesses abide within oneself," light from these three places naturally pacifies all obstacles of the outer, inner, and secret levels. `tsultrim-namdak:c-5-3-4` gives an unrelated body-placement, from "another manual": a green syllable TĀM at Tārā's own heart, on an eight-petaled utpala, with the ten-syllable mantra arranged around it clockwise from the right — from which light radiates in the ten directions, makes offering, gathers blessing, and purifies beings, fulfilling what the claim calls the practice's "four life-nails": the nail of view, the nail of focus (the deity), the nail of recitation (the mantra), and the nail of radiating-and-gathering activity. These are two independent instances of the same general convention (mantra syllables placed at body-centers in generation-stage visualization), not a shared statement — recorded separately.

`palden-sherab` gives three of its own claims that each close a longer generation-stage visualization with an instruction to recite the mantra: `palden-sherab:c-3-1-2-2-2` (after visualizing light dispelling ignorance and opening the treasury of the four correct discernments, "one meditates her thus and recites the vidyā-mantra"); `palden-sherab:c-3-1-4-2-1` (a visualization of the golden deity Uṣṇīṣavijayā — not Tārā herself — closing "then recite the mantra, thus it is said"); and `palden-sherab:c-3-1-20-2-2` (an extended iconographic visualization of Forest-Dwelling Tārā, closing with the instruction to "recite this vidyā-mantra doubling HARA and TUTTĀRA," the formula given in full). No other commentary's claims in the packet give this recite-after-visualize instruction as its own recurring pattern across multiple verses.
