**Tārā (Drolma)** — regarding the etymology of the name, three different scholars agree
in explaining it as: she who delivers and protects sentient beings from the ocean of
suffering, or from the suffering of saṃsāra.⟦1⟧⟦2⟧⟦3⟧

## Etymology (ངེས་ཚིག)

As for the etymology of the name "Drolma": three different scholars explain it in
agreement — as delivering from the ocean of suffering; or as acting to deliver all
sentient beings to the level of buddhahood ⟨the Tibetan reads ས་ལས་, literally "from
the buddha-level"; the intended sense is evidently "to" it — an awkwardness inherited
from the claim's own wording⟩; or as protecting from the suffering of saṃsāra. All
three agree on the meaning of the name.⟦1⟧⟦2⟧⟦3⟧

## Characteristics (མཚན་ཉིད།)

In the commentary composed by the First Dalai Lama, Gendün Drub, separate alternative
names are given for the Tārās of several verses of the praise: for verse 2, "White Tārā
of Radiant Countenance"; for verse 11, "Tārā Who Accomplishes Bliss" and also "Tārā Who
Proclaims the Sound of HŪṂ"; for verse 17, "Tārā Who Bestows the Supreme
Empowerment."⟦1⟧×4 ⟨the renderer emits one ref per supporting passage; four passages
from the same ṭīkā back this sentence, so the same citation appears four times — a
cosmetic point for the pre-publication review⟩

For the name of the ninth verse of praise, both the Geluk master Gendün Drub and the
Nyingma master Khenchen Palden Sherab agree in calling her "Tārā of the Khadira Forest"
(Khadiravaṇī Tārā).⟦1⟧⟦4⟧

Khenchen Palden Sherab also calls the Tārā of the tenth verse "the Venerable Noble Tārā
Who Dispels Poverty and Bestows Wealth."⟦4⟧

## Summary (བསྡུས་དོན།)

On Tārā's history, two Geluk commentaries agree in relating: long ago, in the era of
the Tathāgata "Drum-Sound" (Dundubhisvara), a king's daughter named Yeshe Dawa ("Moon
of Wisdom") presented great offerings to that buddha and generated the resolve for
awakening. Then, by the power of her accomplishing the samādhi that delivers beings
from saṃsāra, her former name Yeshe Dawa became "Drolma," the Deliverer.⟦3⟧⟦5⟧

One Geluk commentary states that if, at the moment one recalls Tārā's qualities and
the occasion arrives ⟨phrase is slightly compressed in the Tibetan⟩, one prays with
one-pointed mind, she protects from the eight fears and the sixteen fears.⟦3⟧

## The schools' respective explanations (གཞུང་ལུགས་སོ་སོའི་བཤད་པ།)

Sermé Tsang Geshé Tendzin Dönzang states: although the Praise in Twenty-One Homages is
generally famed as a praise of Khadiravaṇī Tārā, chief of all the Tārās, when analyzed
in detail it comes down to a praise of the twenty-one Tārās.⟦5⟧

On this point, some scholars hold that all twenty-one Tārās are emanations of
Khadiravaṇī Tārā;⟦5⟧

while certain other scholars hold that the twenty-one Tārās are twenty-one distinct
families — and the two positions disagree.⟦5⟧

# English translation — འཇིག་རྟེན་གསུམ (The Three Worlds)

> **Review aid, not a pipeline artifact.** Translated from `article.wiki` by Claude
> (claude-fable-5, 2026-08-02). ⟦n⟧ marks the article's citations; source key at the
> end. Translator's notes in ⟨angle brackets⟩. This is the run's most substantive
> article: 34 citations across all 16 commentaries, including a genuine
> textual-criticism debate and tantric "hidden meaning" readings.

**The three worlds** — according to the common explanation — means the three
receptacle-worlds stacked in sequence from below to above: below the earth, upon the
earth, and above the earth. Some also explain that nāgas dwell below the earth, humans
upon the earth, and gods above it. The commentaries agree that the protector of the
three worlds is the Noble Avalokiteśvara.⟦3⟧⟦6⟧⟦7⟧⟦8⟧⟦9⟧⟦2⟧⟦10⟧⟦11⟧⟦12⟧⟦13⟧⟦14⟧

## Etymology (ངེས་ཚིག)

The etymology of "the three worlds": it is understood as the three receptacle-worlds
stacked from below upward — below the earth, on the earth, above the earth — this
being the common explanatory tradition.⟦3⟧⟦6⟧⟦7⟧⟦8⟧⟦9⟧⟦2⟧⟦10⟧⟦11⟧⟦12⟧

Many commentaries relate: although Avalokiteśvara had established beings in the state
of buddhahood, he saw that the realms of beings were inexhaustible, and tears fell
from his eyes. From them a lotus flower grew, and from its heart Tārā appeared. On
this basis Tārā is called "she who arose from the heart of the lotus that is the face
of the Protector of the Three Worlds" ⟨the opening image of the praise's first
homage⟩.⟦6⟧⟦7⟧⟦8⟧⟦9⟧⟦13⟧⟦14⟧

From the recitation of names in the tenth homage, Tārā is renowned as "she who brings
the world under her power, the Tārā victorious over the three worlds," explained as
meaning that she possesses the power to overwhelm all three worlds.⟦4⟧⟦5⟧⟦12⟧⟦9⟧⟦15⟧⟦15⟧

## Characteristics (མཚན་ཉིད།)

Many commentaries agree in explaining the words of the seventeenth homage, "she who
shakes the three worlds": by stamping her feet upon Mount Meru, Mount Mandara, and
Mount Vindhya, Tārā shakes even the three worlds. Here too the three worlds are
glossed as the enumeration below-earth / on-earth / above-earth.⟦2⟧⟦5⟧⟦3⟧⟦1⟧⟦9⟧⟦9⟧⟦14⟧⟦10⟧⟦15⟧

Dombuwa Pema Nampar Gyalwa explains in detail that this shaking activity of the
seventeenth verse operates by way of ten aspects.⟦14⟧

Pema Nampar Gyalwa also designates those three worlds by way of karma and result: the
lower realms, the human abode, and the higher realms.⟦14⟧⟦14⟧

## Divisions (དབྱེ་བ།)

The Nyingma Khenchen Palden Sherab explains that the object of the shaking in the
seventeenth verse extends beyond the three mountains — the abodes of the ordinary
great gods — to the three realms of desire, form, and formlessness, or alternatively
to the three worlds of below-earth, on-earth, above-earth, thereby generating many
levels of understanding.⟦4⟧

## The schools' respective explanations (གཞུང་ལུགས་སོ་སོའི་བཤད་པ།)

In a commentary whose colophon is unclear ⟨anonymous; the pipeline preserves the
dkar-chag's attribution "colophon unclear" rather than guessing an author⟩, two
understandings of the seventeenth verse's three worlds are recorded: first, the three
worlds of nāgas below the earth, gods above it, and humans upon it; second, following
Candrakīrti's commentary on the *Four Hundred*, it may also apply to the three realms
— desire and the rest.⟦13⟧

That same commentary firmly refutes glossing the three worlds of the seventeenth verse
as below-earth, above-earth, on-earth.⟦13⟧

The reason: elsewhere in this very text, "the three worlds" is clearly applied to
Meru, Mandara, and Vindhya, and [the other gloss] would contradict that. On this basis
the commentary explains there is no certainty that "the three worlds" must be fixed
exclusively as below-earth and the rest.⟦13⟧

The same commentary further explains that the earlier, middle, and later passages
⟨of the praise⟩ all agree internally in reading "three worlds," while "seven worlds"
occurs nowhere — clearly recording that divergent traditions of translating this line
as "three" versus "seven" exist.⟦13⟧

Finally, since no source for "seven worlds" is found in any sūtra or treatise apart
from this praise, that commentary establishes the reading "three worlds" as the more
plausible translation.⟦13⟧

The Geluk scholar Könchok Tabkhé, however, explains that this divergence between the
"three" and "seven" translations need not be faulted as either a translation error or
a scribal error, and commits to neither.⟦3⟧

The anonymous commentary, citing the words of Master Sūryagupta, also records a
further point of doubt: if Tārā is seated in vajra posture, how exactly are the three
— or seven — worlds pressed beneath her feet?⟦13⟧

The Nyingma Khenchen Palden Sherab, by way of the hidden meaning (sbas don),
interprets both the seventeenth verse's three mountains and its three worlds as the
key channels of the body: Meru is the central channel; the other two mountains are the
great right and left channels; and the three worlds are counted as the three — or six
— channels by the division of upper and lower.⟦4⟧

Ultimately, in terms of the signless completion stage, he also explains the shaking of
the three mountains and three worlds as a symbol of the spontaneously present essence
of the three kāyas being freed in the expanse of one's own awareness.⟦4⟧

The Jonang master Tāranātha likewise, by way of the hidden meaning, interprets Meru as
the spinal column ("Brahmā's bone") and the other two mountains as the two great
channels called Yid-bzang-ma and Dung-can-ma ⟨rasanā and lalanā⟩, a reading the
article notes as agreeing very closely with Khenchen Palden Sherab's tradition.⟦16⟧

# English translation — སྡུག་བསྔལ (Suffering)

**Suffering** — in the context of the meaning of Tārā's name, the scholars agree in
explaining [the name] as protecting sentient beings from suffering, or delivering them
from the ocean of suffering. Regarding the words "Blissful One, Virtuous One, Peaceful
One" (bde ma dge ma zhi ma) of the fifteenth homage as well, many commentaries agree
in explaining: "Blissful" means the absence or exhaustion of resultant suffering;
"Virtuous" means the abandonment of its non-virtuous causes; "Peaceful" means the
exhaustion of the latent traces of the two obscurations through the wisdom that
realizes emptiness — or the truth of the path.⟦1⟧⟦2⟧⟦3⟧⟦4⟧ …

## Etymology (ངེས་ཚིག)

As for the very meaning of Tārā's name: the commentaries agree in explaining it as
protecting sentient beings from suffering, or delivering them from the ocean of
suffering.⟦1⟧⟦2⟧⟦3⟧⟦4⟧

From the recitation of names in the nineteenth homage, Tārā is renowned, by agreement,
as the Tārā who burns away — or clears away — all suffering.⟦9⟧⟦9⟧⟦10⟧

## Characteristics (མཚན་ཉིད།)

Many commentaries agree in explaining the fifteenth homage's "Blissful One, Virtuous
One, Peaceful One": "Blissful" is the absence or exhaustion of resultant suffering;
"Virtuous" is the abandonment of non-virtuous causes; "Peaceful" is the exhaustion of
the latent traces of the two obscurations through the wisdom realizing emptiness or
the truth of the path.⟦1⟧⟦2⟧⟦5⟧⟦4⟧⟦6⟧⟦7⟧⟦3⟧⟦8⟧⟦8⟧

Sermé Tsang Geshé Tendzin Dönzang glosses the meaning of "Blissful One" threefold:
liberation from the lower realms; freedom from physical illness and mental suffering;
and the attainment of undefiled bliss.⟦10⟧

Dorlop Tenga Tulku explains that suffering and fear arise, provisionally, by way of
the four māras: the māra of the divine youth, the māra of the Lord of Death, the māra
of the aggregates, and the māra of the afflictions.⟦5⟧

## Divisions (དབྱེ་བ།)

Tendzin Dönzang divides suffering into three: the suffering of suffering, the
suffering of change, and the all-pervasive suffering of conditioning — and also sets
out six further defects of saṃsāra.⟦10⟧

He also enumerates suffering as eightfold: birth, aging, sickness, death, meeting the
unpleasant, parting from the pleasant, not finding what one seeks though one searches,
and the appropriated aggregates.⟦10⟧

He holds the all-pervasive suffering of conditioning to be the subtlest object of
renunciation: until weariness toward this suffering arises, genuine weariness with
saṃsāra will not come.⟦10⟧

Khenpo Tsültrim Namdak explains the essential meaning of the Śrāvaka vehicle's view,
meditation, and conduct by way of the four truths: suffering, origin, cessation, and
path.⟦8⟧

## The schools' respective explanations (གཞུང་ལུགས་སོ་སོའི་བཤད་པ།)

One commentary also records an explanation by certain other scholars: the three —
Blissful, Virtuous, Peaceful — together with Cessation as the fourth, are interpreted
through the four paths (accumulation, joining, seeing, and no-more-learning);
"Peaceful" then means that when the path of seeing is attained, the sufferings of
birth, aging, sickness, and death are pacified.⟦1⟧ ⟨the claims table deliberately
records this as "one commentary" reporting others' view; the citation identifies the
commentary as Gendün Drub's ṭīkā⟩

A commentary whose colophon is unclear interprets the three — Blissful, Virtuous,
Peaceful — as a graded sequence: freeing the disciples, each according to their karmic
share, from the sufferings of the lower realms, the sufferings of existence, and the
faults of both existence and peace.⟦9⟧

The Nyingma Khenchen Palden Sherab, in the context of the ultimate, signless
completion stage, explains: when dharmatā — awareness-emptiness, unimpeded — becomes
manifest, all dualistic grasping and all hope and fear regarding saṃsāra and nirvāṇa,
good and bad, suffering and bliss, are self-liberated.⟦4⟧

Dorlop Tenga Tulku also sets out a sevenfold division of birthplaces: sentient beings
dwelling in all seven births — the three lower realms and the abodes of gods and
humans in the higher realms — are protected from provisional and ultimate
suffering.⟦5⟧⟦5⟧

Tendzin Dönzang explains that since the afflictions are merely adventitious and have
not entered the nature of mind, whichever path one relies upon, suffering can be
exhausted and the truth of cessation attained.⟦10⟧

Tendzin Dönzang explains the bodhisattva's aspiration as generating the vast intention
to establish all sentient beings not yet relieved of suffering in nirvāṇa — the state
of buddhahood.⟦10⟧
