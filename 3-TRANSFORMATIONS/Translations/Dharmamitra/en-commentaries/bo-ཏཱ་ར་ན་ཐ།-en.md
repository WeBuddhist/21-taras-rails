# An Explanation of the Praise in Twenty-One Homages ^0

## Expression of homage ^I-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^I-1]]

Namo Lokeśvarāya! ^I-1

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^I-2]]

Tārā, who liberates from all cyclic existence,
To her, I prostrate with devotion,
And explain a little of the meaning of the tantra of this praise.
May you pacify all obstacles without exception. ^I-2

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^I-3]]

Regarding this, this tantra is an Unexcelled Yoga Tantra, and furthermore, it is a Yoginī Tantra. Among the six families, it belongs to the family of the Vajra Horse-Supreme, the King of Horses. From that, it is the tantra of the Bhagavatī Tārā, the sovereign of the wheel of the family; this praise is also considered to be a complete tantra in its own right. ^I-3

## The actual praise ^1-0

### First homage ^1-1-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-1]]

Regarding the first verse, "Homage to Tārā, the swift, the heroic," and so forth, the fact that "Homage" appears at the beginning serves as the foundation for initiating the discourse, and this should be understood to apply to the subsequent verses as well. To whom is the homage paid? To the Bhagavatī Tārā. What is the nature of her specific qualities? Because she acts for the benefit of sentient beings swiftly through a great compassion that is free from conceptual grasping, she is "Mother"; because she protects from all fears, she is "Heroic," possessing unobstructed power in her enlightened activity; and because she swiftly generates wisdom-eyes that are like lightning in a single moment, she is the knower who sees all that is to be known. Therefore, she is "Tārā," possessing the enlightened activity of liberating all sentient beings from cyclic existence. Thus, by indicating these—wisdom, love, power, and enlightened activity—it is possible to signify all boundless qualities; therefore, it should be understood that these two words are a summary of everything. ^1-1

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-2]]

This goddess, who possesses such qualities, emanated and arose from the opening of the stamens of the lotus that is the face of Avalokiteśvara, the protector of the three worlds. Regarding this, the translation revised by the translator Nam mkha' bzang po, "Born from the opening of the stamens of the lotus face of the protector of the three worlds," is easy to understand. The non-literal meaning is that the protector of the three worlds is the Dharmakāya, and she who arises from that is the Rūpakāya. ^1-2

### Second homage ^1-2-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-3]]

Regarding the second verse, "Homage to her whose face is like the autumn moon," the autumn moon is completely full, like a hundred full moons piled together, and her face is beautiful and extremely clear, resembling the light of a heap of fire. She is the one whose body is blazing with light that is spread out and radiating, like a gathering of thousands of stars; her body color is white, clear, and captivating, radiating white light. The hidden meaning is that the bodhicitta, like the autumn moon, is piled up without moving from the jewel at the crown of the head, and the fact that all the minor channels are completely filled is like the gathering of stars. ^1-3

### Third homage ^1-3-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-4]]

Regarding the third verse, "Homage to her whose hand is beautifully adorned with a gold and blue water-born lotus," you, the goddess who holds in your hand a lotus that is blue and has a golden stem, are one who has attained mastery over the six pāramitās: generosity, diligence, austerity—which is ethical discipline—peace—which is wisdom—patience, and meditative concentration. Thus, the pāramitās are your very sphere of activity, and you, the goddess, are one who practices them. The hidden meaning is that the lotus, which is the place where the water-born flower resides, and the mudrā of the hand, which is the vajra—that is, the hands joined in equality—represent ethical discipline and austerity that do not waver, meditative concentration that is blissful, wisdom that pacifies conceptual thought through the realization of emptiness, diligence that is unceasing in that, patience that levels all happiness and suffering within that state, and generosity that has abandoned self-grasping; this is the teaching of the practice of mantra. ^1-4

### Fourth homage ^1-4-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-5]]

Regarding the fourth verse, "Homage to the uṣṇīṣa of the Tathāgatas," the emanation of the wisdom of the vidyā-mantra—which possesses infinite benefits and acts to be completely victorious over all opposing forces—arises from the uṣṇīṣa of the Tathāgata Vairocana. You, the virtuous one, having taken this as your own, enjoy it; you ensure the vidyā-mantra for countless sentient beings, and even the sons of the Victorious Ones, the lords of the ten grounds who have attained the ten pāramitās without remainder, rely upon and worship you with reverence. Regarding the profound meaning, the ground of joy in the order of arising and the order of reversal is as stated: "The glorious one who has thus gone, and who has thus gone again," which is the Tathāgata. ^1-5

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-6]]

By the dissolution of the collection of elements and winds into the uṣṇīṣa, one is completely victorious over infinite things to be abandoned, and by the transformation of the ten pāramitās—that is, the ten winds—the sons of the Victorious Ones, the Bodhisattvas, and the sense bases such as the eyes are also transformed into wisdom. This, along with the aforementioned "autumn moon" and so forth, constitutes the limbs of meditative concentration. ^1-6

### Fifth homage ^1-5-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-7]]

Regarding the fifth verse, "Homage to tuttāra," by the sound of the mantra of tuttāra and hūṃ, you pervade and fill all the abodes of the desire realm of this world, the sky—that is, the abodes of the form and formless realms—and by the term "directions," all other world-systems of the ten directions as well. You bring the seven worlds under your control, as if pressing them beneath your feet, and by the power of the vidyā-mantra that you have blessed, you possess the might to summon even great deities such as Maheśvara to serve as your servants; what need is there to mention others of lesser status? Regarding the "seven worlds," although there are many different ways of explaining them, such as the seven of the vessel, the seven of the sentient beings, or the seven of the combined vessel and sentient beings, here they refer to the seven powerful worlds: the worlds of the nāgas, pretas, asuras, humans, vidyādharas, kiṃnaras, and gods. ^1-7

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-8]]

The hidden meaning is that tuttāra is "torment," meaning to be tormented by the fire of caṇḍālī. The syllable hūṃ is the sound of the indestructible, which arises from caṇḍālī. "Desire" refers to the secret cakra, and "directions" refers to the inside of the avadhūtī, which is filled by that sound; the "sky" that is filled is that which is beyond expression. The "seven worlds" are the five nāḍī-cakras and the two cakras of fire and wind, making seven, which are filled by the kuṇḍa that melts from the blazing of caṇḍālī, and that very kuṇḍa is then summoned back, without remainder, from the secret cakra up to the crown of the head. ^1-8

### Sixth homage ^1-6-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-9]]

Regarding the sixth verse, "Homage to Indra, etc.," Indra is the king of the divine realms. Agni is the chief of the sages. Brahmā is considered the creator of the world. Vāyu is the one who establishes the specific locations and arrangements of the world. Rudra, Kāmadeva, the sage Sūrya, and various others who are renowned as creators or īśvaras are the "various īśvaras," and even those who are renowned as the greatest in the world have become objects of worship for you. Bhūtas are the hosts of gaṇapati and other such obstructors and misleading spirits. Vetālas are named after the work they perform, as they are those who perform the work of vetālas; they are of the class of rākṣasas who have attained the power of mantra and are the nine terrifying ones and others who possess great miraculous powers. Gandharvas are those such as the various citraratha, yakṣas are the lords of treasure such as Vaiśravaṇa, and the piśācas are the gods and demons who steal one's radiance; even those hosts who are difficult to tame offer praise before you, meaning that all beings praise you as their supreme guide. ^1-9

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-10]]

The hidden meaning is that the five, beginning with Indra, are the five elements: Indra is earth, Agni is fire, Brahmā is water, Vāyu is wind, and the various īśvaras are space. In this way, the dissolution of the winds of the five elements into the bindu is the "worship." Bhūtas are the channels, vetālas are the bindu, gandharvas are the winds, and yakṣas are the conceptual thoughts; the dissolution of these into the bindu is the "praise from before." By this, the branch of dissolution is taught. ^1-10

### Homage to the Seventh ^1-7-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-11]]

Regarding the seventh verse, "Homage to traṭ," and so forth, the secret mantras of traṭ and phaṭ, and the like, are vidyā-mantras spoken by the Noble Tārā. Through the power of these wrathful practices, one becomes capable of performing various fierce activities; however, this is for the sake of subduing those who are difficult to tame for the benefit of others, and it is not a practice of causing harm. Through certain particulars of such vidyā-mantras, the power of the magical devices and mantras of opposing sorcerers is repelled and destroyed. Furthermore, by manifesting in a wrathful form and pressing down upon the malicious with feet positioned in the manner of right-bent and left-extended, she pacifies epidemics, contagious diseases, and the like. By displaying fire blazing and intensely flaming from her body, which is agitated by wrath, she causes spirits such as yama to scatter, and she performs a great protection for all beings. ^1-11

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-12]]

The hidden meaning is that traṭ signifies pacification, and phaṭ signifies splitting, which is to say, the destruction of conceptual thought. The magical devices of others, along with the winds of the twelve time-conjunctions of karma and kleshas, are blocked; the right channel, ro-ma, is drawn in, meaning its opening faces upward, and the left channel, rkyang-ma, is extended, meaning its opening faces downward. Pressing these two down completely with the foot of the dbu-ma (central channel) signifies that the winds of those two are blocked within the dbu-ma. From that, the fire of gtum-mo blazes, the water of the melting haṃ syllable is agitated, and again, it blazes intensely and melts greatly, and so forth; these are the limbs of srog-rtsol (breath control). ^1-12

### Homage to the Eighth ^1-8-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-13]]

By the power of reciting the rig-sngags (mantra) of oṃ tu-re and so forth for the eighth verse, "Homage to Tu-re," and so on, Tārā herself manifests from the basic space of natural peace into the form of the Great Fearsome One. By this, she destroys the champions of Māra, that is, the military commanders, and by causing her lotus face to possess a frown, she slays the enemies who are hostile to the teachings, who harm many sentient beings, and who create obstacles for the practitioner; this means that they will be without the intention or the means to cause death or harm. ^1-13

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-14]]

Regarding the inner meaning of that: destroying the champions of Māra is the conduct of being completely victorious over all directions. The frown signifies that she has entered into the divine attire, which also signifies the attainment of the wisdom body. The wisdom of non-dual bliss and emptiness is the "Great Fearsome One" because it causes those of inferior capacity to tremble. Through those two—the wisdom and the wisdom body—the enemies, namely the obscurations, are destroyed without exception, and by "Tu-re," meaning quickly, one traverses the higher paths and attains complete buddhahood. These are the twelve grounds, such as kun-tu-'od (the ground of universal light), which are the fruits of the limbs of meditative concentration. ^1-14

### Homage to the Ninth ^1-9-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-15]]

Regarding the ninth verse, "Homage to the Three Jewels," and so on: the utpala mudrā, which symbolizes the Three Jewels, is Tārā’s samaya mudrā. It is adorned at the heart by joining the middle fingers of the cupped hands, resting the index fingers against them, and aligning the thumbs to touch at the tips. If followers also perform this samaya mudrā correctly, the purpose of being blessed by the deity of the rig-sngags (mantra) is fulfilled, as has occurred in the past. This refers to the physical posture of the Bhagavatī herself when performing such a mudrā, and the "wheel of the directions"—meaning the entire expanse of the ten directions—being adorned and pervaded by the turbulent and radiating masses of light rays that arise from her own body. For the follower, if one invokes her with that mudrā, light rays descend from the realms of the ten directions, and she appears in person. ^1-15

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-16]]

The hidden meaning is that the Three Jewels represent the three of khu (semen), rdul (ovum), and rlung (wind); because they are gathered into one, the mudrā of the three fingers and so forth is fully bound at the thig-le (essence) of the central navel, which is the heart of each respective rtsa-’khor-lo (channel-wheel). By this, the wisdom body, formed from the light rays of gtum-mo (inner heat), pervades all directions, which is the meaning of attaining it; through the power of holding [this], mindfulness arises. ^1-16

### Homage to the Tenth ^1-10-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-17]]

Regarding the tenth verse, "Homage to her who is radiant with supreme joy," and so forth: she possesses a body that generates supreme joy for sentient beings, and she is the one who, through the jewel of her crown ornament, spreads and radiates garlands of light. With her laughter and great laughter, and with the sound of the mantra such as Oṃ Tuttāre, she brings even the maras who subjugate other worlds under her power; having also brought all other worlds under her power through her potency, she establishes them in liberation and omniscience. ^1-17

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-18]]

The hidden meaning is that the crown ornament of light rays, which is the radiance of the supreme joy that is coemergent, is the coemergent [bliss] that relies upon the crown of the head; the laughter and great laughter also signify the fire of gtum-mo (inner heat) accompanied by sound. Tuttāre is the "remover of suffering," and because the fire of gtum-mo subdues, it blesses and brings under control the maras of attachment and conceptualization, as well as the body, speech, and mind of the world, through the primordial wisdom of great bliss; this is the limb of mindfulness. ^1-18

### Homage to the Eleventh ^1-11-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-19]]

Regarding the eleventh verse, "Homage to her who has the power to summon all the hosts of local guardians," and so forth: she is the one who possesses the power to summon and bring under her control all the hosts of kings who protect the earth, the earth goddesses, and so forth, as well as the subterranean nāgas, asuras, vidyādharas, and hosts of ḍākinīs, by means of her vidyā-mantra. Through the power of her "vibrating frown"—that is, the wrathful vidyā-mantra accompanied by the syllable Hūṃ—she liberates all destitute beings from the entirety of their suffering of poverty and destitution, pacifying it, and causes the perfection of enjoyment and the great treasure of accomplishments, and so forth, to flourish. ^1-19

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-20]]

The hidden meaning is that by the sound of "local guardians" and "lords of the subterranean," one summons the essence of the elements in the manner of relying upon them from the uṣṇīṣa down to the maṇi; and by the sound of "vibrating frown" and hūṃ, the essence of the "hare-marked" (the moon) is the "frown," which, passing through the tilaka at the center of the eyebrows and becoming stable up to the uṣṇīṣa, is the liberation from poverty for the sake of accomplishing meditative absorptions such as the "sky-treasury." This is the characteristic of the limb of meditative absorption, which is the manner of blocking the movement of the white and red [elements] in the upper and lower [channels]. ^1-20

### Homage to the Twelfth ^1-12-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-21]]

Regarding the twelfth verse, "Homage to her whose head is adorned with a crescent moon," and so forth: the "crescent moon" refers to her being adorned with a half-moon as a crown ornament, and because rays of light blaze intensely from all her other ornaments as well, she is the one who subdues the arrogance of the great lords of wealth, the hundreds of millions. By manifesting at times in the form of an ascetic, she is the one who, from the midst of her rising matted locks, where Amitābha resides and dwells, constantly and without interruption radiates a most intense light, illuminating everything with the radiance of that light. ^1-21

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-22]]

The hidden meaning is to be applied to the preceding context: the "crescent moon" crown ornament is the bodhicitta stabilized at the crown of the head. "Residing amidst the matted locks" is the stabilization at the crown in the manner of a shadow. "Amitābha" is the quintessence of the red element. That these two, the white and the red, radiate intense light means that those white and red elements have become the infinite wheels of the maṇḍala of the great seal of empty form. ^1-22

### Homage to the Thirteenth ^1-13-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-23]]

Regarding the thirteenth verse, "Homage to her who dwells within a garland of blazing fire like that at the end of an eon," and so forth: the "end of an eon" refers to the fire of the time of destruction, and she dwells in the midst of a mass of fire. While remaining unmoved from the inner joy of the undefiled, she is joyful through various displays, such as the wrathful posture of the right leg extended and the left bent, which surrounds her on all sides, and she completely destroys the armies of enemies, that is, the hosts of harmful forces consisting of humans and non-humans. ^1-23

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-24]]

The hidden meaning is: the caṇḍālī wisdom fire is like the blazing fire at the end of time. The flow that arises, pervading the entire body and all appearances, is the "right leg extended and left bent," meaning the right and left ro and kyang channels, or the upper and lower life-sustaining and downward-moving winds, which surround on all sides. This refers to the various manifestations of her play, such as the manner in which the five elements dissolve, each occurring within their respective energy centers. By "joy," it is meant that because the winds have become the drop of bliss, she destroys the enemy armies of the māras and the afflictions. ^1-24

### Homage to the Fourteenth ^1-14-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-25]]

Regarding the fourteenth verse, "Homage to her who strikes the surface of the earth," and so forth: she strikes the surface of the great earth, which includes Mount Meru and the continents, with the palm of her hand. By striking it with her feet and assuming a wrathful, frowning countenance, she uses the power of her wrath to proclaim the terrifying sound of "Hūṃ" from her nose, thereby shattering all the malicious nāgas, asuras, rākṣasas, and pretas dwelling in the seven levels beneath the earth, causing them to be scattered into pieces. ^1-25

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-26]]

As for the seven levels beneath that: beneath this very great earth, there exist seven successive levels of great realms of non-human beings. Although some say that these are measured downwards from the hollow space at the summit of Mount Meru, this does not appear to be correct. They are called the Basis, the Superior Basis, the Baseless, the Basis Itself, the Basis of Essence, the Good Basis, and the Pure Basis. These are the great worlds beneath the earth. ^1-26

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-27]]

The hidden meaning is as follows: the surface of the earth is the mandala of the navel, which is struck by the feet of the goddess—who is the definitive meaning—and pressed by her hands of flame, through the fire of caṇḍālī wisdom. These seven levels beneath the earth are the seven coils of the caṇḍālī fire, which resembles a coiled serpent, residing at the lower end of the avadhūtī. When the bodhicitta of the blazing caṇḍālī arrives there, the torment of the fire is completely pacified, and the knots of the channels are shattered and destroyed. ^1-27

### Fifteenth Homage ^1-15-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-28]]

Regarding the fifteenth verse, "Homage to the Blissful, Virtuous, and Peaceful One," and so forth: the Blessed One herself is always blissful, virtuous, and of a peaceful nature. Because she is in the state of nirvāṇa, which is supreme peace, and remains in equipoise within the expanse of the truth of cessation, she is one whose sphere of experience is peace. Therefore, she generates the happiness of this life for sentient beings, as well as virtue for the sake of the hereafter. For those who are fortunate, she teaches the path of peace and establishes them in the sphere of experience of the supreme peace of nirvāṇa, which is the fruit of non-abiding. Furthermore, by reciting the vidyā-mantra spoken by the goddess herself, which is perfectly endowed with svāhā and oṃ, she also destroys great negativity and the great suffering that is its fruit. By this, the svāhā that concludes the mantra for the activity of pacification is explained; and since it was stated above that traṭ is for repelling and phaṭ is for killing, the conclusion of the mantras for other activities is also incidentally indicated. ^1-28

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-29]]

The ultimate definitive meaning of this is: the Blissful One is the wisdom of discernment; the Virtuous One is the mirror-like wisdom; the Peaceful One is the wisdom of equality; the one who is peaceful in nirvāṇa is the wisdom of the expanse of phenomena; and the one who destroys great negativity is the all-accomplishing wisdom. Because the indestructible sound of the speech, represented by the vidyā-mantra oṃ and svāhā, arises boundlessly, she performs the benefit of sentient beings, such as pacifying negativity, which is to say that this is applied to the result. ^1-29

### Sixteenth Homage ^1-16-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-30]]

Regarding the sixteenth verse, "Homage to Her who is surrounded by those of intense joy and utterly shatters the bodies of enemies," those who are vidyādhara adepts, bodhisattvas, and those possessing the samādhi and the mind of intense joy surround Her. She utterly shatters—that is, disperses—the bodies of enemies, such as the sinful lord of the Paranirmitavaśavartin realm, who are harmful to sentient beings. You are the Goddess who has emerged from the arrangement of the ten-syllable speech, the mantra. You are the Illuminator, the one whose body blazes with light, who has emerged from the vidyā-mantra—the vidyā—of the syllable HŪṂ. By this, the wrathful Tārā is shown to have the seed syllable HŪṂ and the ten-syllable mantra of approach. ^1-30

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-31]]

The definitive meaning is: through the light rays of the empty-form Mahāmudrā, one is surrounded by intense joy, which is the unchanging bliss; by this, one destroys the enemy, which is the body of impure appearance. This refers to the branch of meditative concentration. The ten syllables are the ten signs obtained from the cessation of the parts of the ten mandalas of the right and left [channels]. Furthermore, the syllables represent the winds, and the etymological meaning of the syllable, akṣara, is "unchanging," meaning the ten appearances of the unchanging expanse of phenomena, or the ten parts of unchanging bliss that arise from perceiving them. ^1-31

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-32]]

Regarding the sign of the vidyā HŪṂ: the five-part HŪṂ represents the five wisdoms, which are the bindus of the nature of the five wisdoms, together with the reflections of the three realms. These are established as the phenomena of those ten signs because they arise from filling the central channel with wind. Remaining in equipoise regarding this is the supreme vidyā-mantra of ultimate reality, and the "Illuminator" is the clear appearance of empty form. This is the yoga of withdrawal. ^1-32

### Seventeenth Homage ^1-17-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-33]]

The meaning of the seventeenth verse, "Homage to Ture," and so forth, is as follows: "Ture" means "the swift one," signifying the goddess who acts swiftly. By stamping her two feet in a wrathful manner, she causes the three worlds—the subterranean, the terrestrial, and the celestial—to tremble, along with the king of mountains, Mount Meru, the mountain called Mandara, the mountain called the Snowy Range, and the mountains where the Great Lord dwells. ^1-33

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-34]]

If we follow the reading "Vindhya" (the Piercer) that appears in most Tibetan editions, then it is the king of mountains, the Piercer. However, in those versions of this text where the Sanskrit is visible, it appears as Kailāśa; if that is the case, it accords with the reading "Snowy Mountain." Because various editions appear, it is difficult to fix upon one, so it should be understood in a way that both are not contradictory. ^1-34

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-35]]

The phrase "the form of HŪṂ" refers to hūṃ-kāra, the syllable HŪṂ. The phrase "in the three worlds" uses the seventh case to indicate the object of the second case, meaning that the action of shaking is applied to the three worlds. If the three worlds are shaken, one might ask, what is the point of mentioning Mount Meru and the others? Although the three worlds in general are shaken, it is possible that those specific places where powerful deities reside might not be shaken; therefore, they are mentioned to signify the great power of the Āryā herself. ^1-35

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-36]]

The hidden meaning is as follows: Mount Meru is the bone of Brahmā; the other two mountains are Yid-bzang-ma and Dung-can-ma; "shaking" means being completely filled with the bodhicitta; the "three worlds" are the six channels, divided into three upper and three lower. Regarding the sign of HŪṂ: it is the avadhūtī, the mind-vajra channel, which is the seed of all channels, and the power is dissolved therein. ^1-36

### Eighteenth Homage ^1-18-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-37]]

Regarding the eighteenth verse, "Homage to her who holds in her hand the deer-marked one, which has the form of a celestial lake," the "celestial lake" refers to a mind-lake, or one that has the appearance of a long, gently flowing stream. The "deer-marked one" is the moon, meaning she holds the moon in her hand. By reciting its vidyā-mantra, "Hare," twice, and with the addition of the syllable "Phaṭ" at the end, the mantra clears away all poisons of the stable and the moving without exception. By this, one meditates on the form of her who holds the moon, and by reciting that specific mantra, one heals and clears away the poisons of snakes and the like. The mantra of reciting "Hare" twice is: OṂ NAMAḤ TĀRE NAMO HARE HŪṂ HARE SVĀHĀ. Appending "SARVA VIJAYA ŚANTIM KURU PHAṬ" to the end of that is the method for clearing poison, which is the action-mantra of the wrathful Tārā. This is the instruction for the subsequent practice. ^1-37

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-38]]

In the definitive meaning: the "celestial" arises from the body, and its "lake" is the coemergent bliss together with all its supports; the primordial wisdom of emptiness, which is inseparable from that and stainless like the moon, is what is meant by "holding in her hand." Through that non-dual bliss and emptiness, "Hara"—which means to take away—is recited twice as a sign to take away the faults of the two obscurations, and by means of the impure body, one subdues poison. ^1-38

### Nineteenth Homage ^1-19-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-39]]

Regarding the nineteenth verse, "Homage to the queen of the hosts of gods," the "hosts of gods" refers to the six classes of gods of the desire realm. Likewise, "king" refers to those who exercise power in this world, such as universal monarchs and others. The mountain deities, tree deities, and lake goddesses, and so forth, who bring benefit or harm in the world, are specific types of gods; these, along with the powerful kinnaras, rely upon your lotus feet with devotion. By the practitioner who is completely bound by the armor of the mantra and mudrā blessed by you, O Goddess, the majesty of body, speech, and mind dispels all disharmony, such as the disputes of others and bad dreams. Here, "armor" means protection. ^1-39

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-40]]

In the definitive meaning: "deva" (god) is derived from the word deva, meaning deha-bhava, that which arises from the body; it is the wisdom body. By mentioning "hosts," it means that this wisdom body possesses many purities, such as the ten aggregates and elements. That same one is also the king of the vidyādharas, and because she is worthy of worship by the three worlds, she is served by gods, kinnaras, and others. The cause of this is the majesty of the armor of the bindu-hosts, bound without wavering, which vanquishes the disputes of changing attachment and the bad dreams of ordinary appearances. ^1-40

### Twentieth Homage ^1-20-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-41]]

Regarding the twentieth verse, "Homage to the one whose two eyes...", when she gazes upon all beings with a vision that is extremely brilliant, like the rays of light fully expanded from the sun and moon, she establishes all beings who are suffering, such as those in the lower realms, in happiness. By reciting the hara mantra twice, along with the tuttāra mantra, she dispels even the most violent and incurable epidemic diseases. In terms of the immediate context, the ten-syllable mantra is taught to pacify epidemic diseases, halt plagues, and protect against contagious illnesses; however, the primary meaning is that by reciting the ten syllables, one pacifies all defilements, which are like violent epidemic diseases. ^1-41

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-42]]

In this context, the two eyes are likened to the sun and moon because the light of the right eye terrifies those who cause harm and burns away sins and suffering, thus it is light like the sun; and because the rays of the left eye bestow life, enjoyment, and happiness, it is the light of the moon that radiates nectar. The phrase "uttering Tāra twice" identifies the two tāre and tuttāre syllables, which is the very same ten-syllable speech mentioned previously. Therefore, it is the complete oṃ tāre tuttāre ture svāhā, which is the mantra for all three—the peaceful, increasing, and wrathful—practices of Tārā. ^1-42

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-43]]

The hidden meaning is that the sun and moon symbols represent the two internal khu-rdul (semen and ovum). The eyes that are completely filled by these are the two mantras at the top and bottom of the avadhūtī, and because primordial wisdom arises from them, they are "brilliantly radiant." The two hara syllables mean "to liberate," signifying liberation from the two extremes of existence and quiescence through bliss and emptiness. Tuttāre means "torment," which is asceticism; by pressing the sun downward and fixing the moon at the crown, this unchanging asceticism dispels the epidemic disease of the shifting bindu. ^1-43

### Twenty-first Homage ^1-21-0

#### First, the literal meaning ^1-21-1-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-44]]

Regarding the twenty-first verse, "Homage to her who is perfectly endowed with the power of peace," and so forth, there are four aspects: the meaning of the syllables, the general meaning, the hidden meaning, and the ultimate meaning. The first is that because the mind of the trainee is perfectly established in the three suchnesses—emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness—through their power, all obscurations are abandoned and one is established in peace; thus, she is the one perfectly endowed with the power to ripen and liberate the trainee. ^1-44

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-45]]

"Spirits" refers to the eighteen types of spirits, such as the nam-gu spirits. "Zombies" refers to the work of zombies, that is, malevolent mantras such as those for casting and sending. "Yakṣas" refers to the harm caused by those who steal one's vitality. All such harm is destroyed by the three gates of liberation, and it is also destroyed through the power of the mantra "supreme ture." Alternatively, ture means "the swift one," signifying that you, the supreme goddess, swiftly destroy all harm. Since it can also be interpreted in this way, it is correct to emend it to "the supreme mother who swiftly destroys." ^1-45

#### Second, the general meaning ^1-21-2-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-46]]

The second, the general meaning, is that by placing the suchness of the three syllables at the three places within the meditative stabilization of generating the Tārā form, it becomes the supreme means for pacification, that is, for guarding against obstacles, and it also teaches the pith instructions for protection against the harm of spirits and the like. ^1-46

#### Third, the hidden meaning ^1-21-3-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-47]]

The third is the hidden meaning: the three suchnesses are appearance, increase, and near-attainment. Once the arrangement of these has dawned in sequence, it proceeds to the pacification—that is, the momentary pacification of all—which is the empty clear light. By destroying the "spirits" (the channels), the "zombies" (the bindus), and the "yakṣas" (the conceptual thoughts) within the expanse of clear light, one swiftly arises in the supreme form of the deity. ^1-47

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-48]]

Regarding a category such as this, the Lamp of Illumination states:
Clear light is the supreme teaching,
And the realization of union is supreme.
The "limit" is explained in two ways.
"Limit" is a term for reaching the final boundary. ^1-48

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-49]]

Although the followers of the Ārya tradition of the Guhyasamāja hold this to be the ultimate explanation, one should also know that, in general, there is a hidden meaning as well. ^1-49

#### Fourth, the ultimate ^1-21-4-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-50]]

The fourth is the ultimate: these three—the vajra of body, speech, and mind—are established from the beginning as the nature of all phenomena. Because that nature is pure from the beginning, one is endowed with the power to attain pacification through the practice of the path, and by resting in equipoise within that very expanse where the three vajras are inseparable, one swiftly destroys the suffering equivalent to the harm of "spirits," the karma equivalent to the actions of "zombies," and the afflictions equivalent to "yakṣas," thereby actualizing the sole, supreme, innate wisdom of great bliss. ^1-50

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-51]]

This is the meaning of that statement. Thus, through these words of the extensive explanation that follow the literal meaning, the praise is made from the perspective of the Bhagavatī as the Emanation Body, who is shown within the mandalas of the retinues of the buddhas, and who is renowned for dwelling manifestly in the abodes of the kings of gods, on Mount Potala, and so forth; yet, in terms of the ultimate meaning, it is also a praise of the Complete Enjoyment Body and the Truth Body. ^1-51

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-52]]

These definitive meanings are explained in accordance with the six-branched Vajra Yoga, the ultimate profound path. This is for the most part explained in agreement with two commentaries composed by Indian scholars on the specific meaning of the Tārā-udbhava-tantra, as well as what appears within the great tantric commentaries of Dīpaṃkara. Regarding those points where there are slight discrepancies in the instructions of Vajra Yoga among them, I have interpreted them in accordance with Vajra Yoga, based upon the ultimate profound meaning of the tantras and following the instruction that one tantra must be understood through another. ^1-52

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-53]]

Furthermore, there are also such categories of necessary items: "autumn moon" and so forth [indicates] generation from the moon and seed-syllable; "sun and moon expanded" and so forth [indicates] generation from the five manifest enlightenments; "Tathāgata's crown protrusion" [indicates] the bestowing of the deity empowerment; "protector of the three worlds" and so forth [indicates] the generation of the three rituals; "three suchnesses" and so forth [indicates] the blessing of the three vajras; "armor on all sides" and so forth [indicates] the blessing of the sense faculties; and "revolving on all sides with great joy" and "right leg extended, left bent, revolving on all sides with joy" [indicates] the retinue of deities. ^1-53

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-54]]

The aspect of the generation stage, and "sun and moon expanded" and so forth [indicates] the rituals; [the practice of] looking with the eyes at colored sand and so forth [indicates] the blessing; "crown ornament of a garland of spreading light" [indicates] the bestowing of the diadem empowerment and so forth; "the syllable Hūṃ" [indicates] the bestowing of the mantra empowerment; "moon-tip crown ornament" [indicates] the secret empowerment; "Amitābha from the midst of the hair" [indicates] the wisdom empowerment; and "joyful one, virtuous one" and so forth [indicates] the fourth empowerment. These are the meanings of the empowerments. ^1-54

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^1-55]]

"The Blazing, Swirling One," "The Garland of Fire," "The One Blazing with Great Light," and "The Lamp Itself" indicate the burnt offerings for accomplishing the four activities and the attainments, and by the bestowing of empowerment, they also indicate the consecration. Furthermore, this text also very clearly indicates the sadhana known as "The Lamp, Peaceful by Day and Wrathful by Night," and it is also the case that the twenty-one sadhanas and the twenty-one auxiliary practices taught here are extremely renowned; I have not elaborated upon them as it would become too extensive. ^1-55

## Concluding meaning ^2-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^2-1]]

Regarding the concluding meaning, the benefits of the praise are taught thereafter. In that regard, firstly, from the two verses beginning with "the root mantra and so forth," it is shown that this is the praise arising from the ten syllables of the root mantra, and that this is also the praise containing the twenty-one "homages." Therefore, the word "and" is a particle of inclusion. ^2-1

### Meaning of the root mantra ^2-1-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^2-2]]

Regarding the meaning of the root mantra: "Oṃ" is the supreme purity, the dharmadhātu, the dharmakāya, the holder of the jewel of all qualities, and the supreme dharma, the essence of the Sugatas. Tāre is the primordial wisdom arising from the dharmakāya; Tuttāre is the body arising from the primordial dharma; Ture is the great compassion arising from the ineffable dharma, representing the emanation and the activity. Svā is self-blessing; Hā is Ārali derived from Ha, meaning "Emaho," the display, which is the infinite body, pure realm, and activity that are like an illusion. Because it praises by means of these meanings, it is a praise by the root mantra. ^2-2

## Explanation of the benefits ^3-0

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-1]]

The object of the action of praise is the Goddess, that is, the Bhagavatī. The agent is one endowed with genuine reverence. The performer is any intelligent person. As for the manner: it is by being endowed with great faith and diligent effort, and by reciting this praise. ^3-1

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-2]]

Having risen at dusk and at dawn—that is, having performed the act of rising—and with prostration and offering preceding it, one should keep the qualities of the Āryā in mind. The benefits of this are that she grants all fearlessness; she protects from all fears, such as the eight great fears and the sixteen fears, and she bestows the perfect mastery of the state of fearlessness. Regarding the phrase "recites with diligence" (rab dang brjod cing), since the Sanskrit word "va" is present, it is correct to translate it as "recites with effort." Therefore, if any intelligent person, through the six sessions from dusk onwards, remains reverent toward the Goddess and, by way of remembering her qualities, recites this praise with effort, these benefits will be present; this is how it should be understood throughout the entire preceding and following text. ^3-2

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-3]]

"Sin" refers to the fruits of sin; all previously existing illnesses, malevolent spirits, misfortunes, and unfortunate destinies are thoroughly pacified, and those that would have arisen again are averted. Likewise, even if one is a person whose karma makes birth in the lower realms certain due to the power of past sinful deeds, through the brilliance of reciting this praise, one attains the state of overcoming all such sinful karma and the births in lower realms; thus, one will not be born in the lower realms. Conversely, it is also taught that one will achieve the perfect attainment of birth in the higher realms; by this, the benefits for both this life and the next within cyclic existence are taught. ^3-3

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-4]]

By reciting the praise to the seventy million Victors and the like, and by remaining in the practice of that, one will swiftly see the faces of the seventy million emanated bodies of the Victors, the Buddhas, who have come and reside together with this Bhagavatī. Having seen them, one will be empowered by them, either in reality or in a dream, through the flowing streams of nectar from their light rays or their hands. ^3-4

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-5]]

This also teaches the water empowerment at the time of ripening the student. By the condition of that empowerment, many obscurations are purified, and many distinct meditative concentrations and dhāraṇīs are attained, such that one gradually attains the siddhi of Mahāmudrā, which possesses many qualities far greater than this perfect assembly. Having attained that, one traverses the stages of the Āryas and ultimately proceeds to the rank of a perfect, complete Buddha. Thus, the benefits of attaining liberation and omniscience are taught. ^3-5

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-6]]

In order to teach other benefits through the power of the mantra in this context, the third verse is spoken, beginning with "Its poison..." Regarding this, there are poisons that are stable, such as the "black-piled" poison, and poisons that are moving, such as those from venomous snakes, carbuncles, and boils. The term "stable" is mentioned alongside "fixed" because it is a synonym, and both are stated for the sake of clarity. It is easy to understand the emendation by Gnyan lo, "Whether it be stable or moving," or by Nam mkha' bzang po, "Whatever is either stable or moving." ^3-6

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-7]]

Such poison is extremely violent, taking life and causing great suffering. That very poison, by merely remembering the vidyā-mantra of Tārā, is completely terrified—that is, its harms are cleared away and it becomes like nectar. Regarding the phrase "even if eaten or drunk," it should be connected before the phrase "completely eliminated through recollection," meaning that even if such poison has been eaten or drunk, or if one has been touched by poisonous ointments and the like, it is eliminated in an instant by remembering the vidyā-mantra. Although in other translations the phrase "eaten or drunk" is placed after the word "recollection," which would lead to it being connected with the following verse, nevertheless, as it appears as khāditaṃ pītam eva ca, the original sequence is correct. ^3-7

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-8]]

Regarding "spirits and so forth," the masses of suffering residing within oneself—such as the harms of various types of spirits, illnesses like fevers, and the torments of poisons—are all thoroughly abandoned through the power of this vidyā-mantra of Tārā, and not only is happiness attained, but that vidyā-mantra practitioner, merely by thinking of it with their mind or by performing the application of a pacifying rite, also causes other sentient beings to abandon that suffering. Likewise, by taking up the final line of the verse for everyone, obstacles are removed and demons and the like are individually vanquished. ^3-8

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-9]]

Regarding "two, three, and seven times," "two" refers to reciting it during the day and at night. "Three" refers to the three sessions, which may be during the day or at night. In each of these, the praise is to be recited seven times in connection with the practice, just as it arises from the guru’s oral instructions. If it is recited in this way, for a householder, "child" refers to one's lineage, and for one who has renounced, it refers to a dharma-child or student; thus, those desiring a child or student will obtain many. Those desiring wealth will obtain wealth and enjoyments. Furthermore, all manner of siddhis will be obtained, such as pacifying rites and the like, as well as pills, eye-salves, swords, yakṣiṇīs, and great treasures. ^3-9

![[bo-ཏཱ་ར་ན་ཐ།#^3-10]]

Regarding the "Praise to the Exalted Tārā," the statement that it was spoken by the perfectly complete Buddha Vairocana means that it originates from the Tārā-mūla-kalpa-nāma-aṣṭaśataka (The Seven-Hundred-Chapter King of Tantras of Tārā). This is what is asserted. Although the text known as the Tantra of Various Rites—which is currently found in this region—also appears, it is the consensus of scholars that it is a composed tantra, and since that position is correct, it does not originate from there. It is not contradictory, however, for it to be a fragment of an extensive tantra or a condensed tantra and so forth. "Completed" (rdzogs so) means samāpta, which is to say, perfectly attained. ^3-10
